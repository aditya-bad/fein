# Why Knowing Investing Principles Does Not Make People Rich: The Knowledge-to-Outcome Gap

Research date: 19 Nov 2026. Most figures come from search-result summaries of the pages cited. Where I could not open a primary document, I say so. Calculations I did myself are marked [CALC].

## 1. Survivorship bias: where big fortunes actually come from, and who used the same strategies and blew up

### Takeaway
Very large fortunes mostly come from founding or owning a business, not from picking public stocks. "Finance & Investments" produces many billionaires, but they are mostly fund *managers* who collect fees and carry on other people's capital, and dealmakers. They are not retail stock pickers. For every visible winner, there are people who ran the same concentrated or leveraged playbook and were wiped out: Archegos, LTCM, Harshad Mehta and Ketan Parekh, Indian small-cap holders in 2008, and Indian F&O traders.

### Cited Findings
**Where wealth comes from**
- Kaplan & Rauh (AER 2013, "Family, Education, and Sources of Wealth among the Richest Americans, 1982–2012"): 69% of the 2011 Forbes 400 started their own business, up from 40% in 1982. The authors credit early access to education, applied in scalable industries (technology, finance, mass retail). — [Stanford GSB](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/joshua-rauh-what-forbes-400-list-says-about-american-wealth); [Chicago Booth Review](https://www.chicagobooth.edu/review/billionaires-self-made); [RePEc](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:aea:aecrev:v:103:y:2013:i:3:p:158-62)
- Forbes' own self-made score put 70.5% of the 2021 Forbes 400 as self-made, but many of those "self-made" people grew up with advantages. — [Chicago Booth Review](https://www.chicagobooth.edu/review/billionaires-self-made)
- Korom, Lutter & Beckert (Max Planck): self-made entrepreneurs in lucrative fields, *e.g., hedge fund managers*, increasingly populate the Forbes 400. Heirs are *more* likely than self-made entrepreneurs to stay on the list, all else equal. This is a survivorship point: inherited, diversified wealth lasts longer than concentrated founder wealth. — [MPIfG paper](https://pure.mpg.de/pubman/item/item_2418682_8/component/file_2632377/SSR_65_2017_Korom.pdf)
- 2026 Forbes billionaires: about 3,428 billionaires, about 65% self-made (vs about 58% in 2010). "Finance & Investments" was reportedly the largest category, with about 74% of finance billionaires self-made. These numbers come from secondary aggregators, and their definitions of "self-made" differ. — [BusinessStats](https://businesstats.com/?p=5511); [247WallSt (only ~8% fully inherited)](https://247wallst.com/personal-finance/2026/09/15/only-8-of-billionaires-fully-inherited-their-money-heres-how-the-rest-got-rich/)
- Bessembinder: about 4% of U.S. listed stocks since 1926 (about 1,092 of about 26,000) account for *all* net wealth creation over T-bills. Five firms account for 11.9% of it. The majority of stocks underperform T-bills over their lifetimes. — [ASU W.P. Carey](https://blogs.wpcarey.asu.edu/20180917-do-stocks-outperform-treasury-bills); [ASU 2024 update](https://blogs.wpcarey.asu.edu/20240805-asu-study-stock-market-winners-uncovers-some-surprises). A 2026 Strathclyde paper calls the figure replicable but says the interpretation is open to debate: should investors hunt for the 4% or own everything? — [Strathclyde](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/97040/1/OConnell-etal-2026-What-does-shareholder-wealth-creation-tell.pdf)

**Same playbook, opposite outcome (leverage and concentration)**
- Archegos / Bill Hwang: the fund managed about $36bn of capital, but derivatives gave it about $160bn of stock exposure. Its 2021 collapse cost banks more than $10bn. Hwang was sentenced to 18 years on 20 Nov 2024 and ordered to pay more than $9bn in restitution. — [DOJ SDNY](https://www.justice.gov/usao-sdny/pr/founder-and-head-archegos-capital-management-bill-hwang-sentenced-18-years-prison); [Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/economy/2024/11/20/archegoss-bill-hwang-sentenced-to-18-years-in-prison-for-massive-us-fraud). **MYTH CHECK:** the often-quoted "$20bn" loss figure is not supported. Reported bank losses are about $10bn.
- LTCM (1998): Nobel laureates Merton and Scholes were on its board. It lost about $4.6bn in under four months, with about $4.7bn of equity against more than $124.5bn of borrowings (over 25:1 debt-to-equity at the start of 1998). Some sources give 20:1, or about 200:1 at the peak, using different measures. — [Wikipedia: LTCM](https://Www.Wikipedia.org/wiki/LTCM); [McGraw-Hill case](https://highered.mheducation.com/sites/dl/free/0077151569/977786/c26i_LTCM.pdf); [Fortune 1998](https://money.cnn.com/magazines/fortune/fortune_archive/1998/10/26/250015/index.htm)
- Buffett on LTCM: "To make money they didn't have and didn't need, they risked what they did have and did need… That is just plain foolish." It is usually attributed to his 1998 University of Florida talk. The wording varies between sources, and I did not verify it against the transcript. — [Novel Investor](https://novelinvestor.com/buffetts-lessons-long-term-capital-management/); [Marginal Revolution](https://marginalrevolution.com/marginalrevolution/2007/04/buffett_on_ltcm.html)
- Harshad Mehta (1992): used fake bank receipts to channel bank money into equities. One estimate says over ₹40,000 crore of market wealth was erased in the crash. SEBI was given statutory power in 1992 in the aftermath. — [Samco](https://www.samco.in/knowledge-center/articles/3-stock-market-scams-that-shook-the-nation/); [Upstox](https://upstox.com/learning-center/share-market/history-of-stock-market-crashes-in-india) (aggregators, so these are lower-quality sources)
- Ketan Parekh (1999–2001): circular trading and pump-and-dump in the "K-10" stocks, arrested by the CBI in 2001, convicted in 2008. — [Wikipedia: Ketan Parekh](https://en.wikipedia.com/wiki/Ketan_Parekh); [iPleaders](https://blog.ipleaders.in/ketan-parekh-scam/)
- Indian small caps: the BSE Smallcap fell about 68% in 2008. The Nifty Smallcap 100's maximum fall that year was about 76%. — [Business Standard](https://business-standard.com/article/markets/is-the-worst-over-for-small-caps-here-s-what-past-data-indicates-118120900518_1.html); [5paisa](https://www.5paisa.com/blog/small-cap-index-30-percent-below-peak-36-percent-trading-days). In 2018 the BSE Smallcap fell about 28% from its January peak to September and 23.5% over the calendar year. 67 index stocks were already down more than 50% by June, and 111 stocks were down more than 50% by late June. — [Business Standard (67 stocks)](https://www.business-standard.com/article/markets/67-stocks-from-bse-smallcap-index-fall-more-than-50-in-2018-118060500249_1.html); [Business Standard (111 stocks)](https://business-standard.com/amp/article/markets/bse-smallcap-index-hits-9-month-low-111-stocks-tank-over-50-in-cy18-118062800350_1.html); [BS year-end](https://www.business-standard.com/article/pti-stories/small-stocks-lose-sheen-tumbles-nearly-24-in-2018-118123100707_1.html). From 2004 onwards, the small-cap index spent 36% of trading days more than 30% below its peak. — [5paisa](https://www.5paisa.com/blog/small-cap-index-30-percent-below-peak-36-percent-trading-days)
- Indian retail derivatives:
  - SEBI study, FY22–FY24: more than 93% of over 1 crore individual F&O traders lost money, an average loss of about ₹2 lakh each. — [Tribune](https://www.tribuneindia.com/news/business/93-retail-traders-lost-money-in-fo-in-3-years-sebi-study/amp)
  - FY25: about 91% lost money, with net losses of ₹1,05,603 crore (vs ₹74,812 crore in FY24). — [Business Standard](https://www.business-standard.com/amp/markets/news/net-losses-of-traders-in-fo-widens-in-fy25-sebi-study-125070701221_1.html); [Value Research](https://www.valueresearchonline.com/stories/225398/average-trader-lost-rs-1-1-lakh-fo-fy25/)
  - FY26 study: 87.7% still lost. Loss rates fall with the size of a trader's equity holdings, from 93% for those with no equity holdings to 58% for those holding more than ₹10 crore. — [CorpLaw Updates](https://www.corplawupdates.in/updates/sebi-equity-derivatives-retail-trader-study-fy26)

### Inferences
- The visible winners are a biased sample. Founders hold one concentrated asset that they *control and operate*. Public-market investors who copy that concentration lack the control and the information, and add leverage. The leveraged, concentrated copycats go bust and drop off the lists, so nobody sees them.
- Rich-list "finance" fortunes come mainly from asset-management *businesses*: fees on other people's capital, scale, and franchise. That is itself a founder or owner route, not personal stock-picking returns.
- SEBI's FY26 finding (losses fall as equity holdings rise) suggests capital base and experience shape outcomes, not just knowledge. Selection effects may also explain it.

### Gaps
- I found no dataset counting Forbes billionaires or Forbes 30 Under 30 honorees whose wealth came *mainly from personal stock-picking*. Rough count from general knowledge, not sourced: Buffett, and arguably Rakesh Jhunjhunwala and Radhakishan Damani in India. Damani's fortune is mainly DMart, a business he founded after trading. Treat this as unsourced.
- I did not open Hurun India methodology data on sources of wealth.
- I found no source quantifying the Indian retail "option shorting went broke" cases beyond SEBI's aggregate loss data.

## 2. Luck vs skill: paradox of skill and how long a record is needed

### Takeaway
Michael Mauboussin's "paradox of skill" says that as competitors become more skilled and more alike, the spread of skill narrows, so luck explains more of the difference in results. In markets, a short record (even 3–5 years) cannot statistically separate skill from luck. Separating them takes 10–15+ years, or decades across cycles.

### Cited Findings
- Paradox of skill: Ted Williams' .406 in 1941 was about 4 standard deviations above average. A 4-sigma hitter in 2011 would bat about .380, because overall skill rose and the variance shrank. — [Advisor Perspectives (Mauboussin interview)](https://www.advisorperspectives.com/articles/2012/12/11/luck-versus-skill-in-investing-new-insights-from-the-world-of-sports-and-beyond)
- The standard deviation of mutual-fund excess returns declined steadily from 1962 to 2008. It was once well above 10% and is now closer to 6%, per Mauboussin & Callahan, "Alpha and the Paradox of Skill". These figures are from secondary summaries. — [Institutional Investor](https://www.institutionalinvestor.com/article/2bsu8copm52a2xoxxejnk/culture/five-questions-mauboussin-explores-why-luck-matters-to-investors); [Daily Economy](https://thedailyeconomy.org/article/why-passive-management-wins-again/); [Morgan Stanley IM, "Dispersion and Alpha Conversion"](https://www.morganstanley.com/im/publication/insights/articles/dispersion-and-alpha-conversion.pdf)
- Mauboussin & Arbesman (2010) studied 5,593 large-cap U.S. active funds from 1962 to 2008 and tested them against 10,000 no-skill simulations. Actual results: 454 streaks of outperformance of at least 5 years, 3 of at least 10 years, and a longest streak of 15 years. That is more than chance predicts, so some skill exists, but it is rare. — [CXO Advisory](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/investing-expertise/outperformance-streaks-and-mutual-fund-manager-skill)
- Damodaran's heuristic: beating the market by 2–3% a year for 15 years with low variability suggests skill. The same average with high variability does not. — [Damodaran Substack](https://aswathdamodaran.substack.com/p/luck-versus-skill-how-can-you-tell-11-03-12)
- Fama–French estimate the standard deviation of *true* alpha across funds at only about 1.25% a year. That is small relative to noise. — cited in [Scribd doc](https://www.scribd.com/document/482122040/document-805456950-pdf) (secondary)

### Inferences
- [CALC, illustrative] Suppose true alpha is 2% a year and tracking error is 8%. The t-statistic is about 0.25 × √N, so reaching t ≈ 2 needs N ≈ 64 years. Even a 4% alpha needs about 16 years. That is why "I beat the market for 3 years" proves almost nothing.
- Knowing the principles is now common, which is exactly what the paradox of skill describes. Shared knowledge raises the average level of skill, shrinks any edge from knowing it, and leaves luck, temperament, and capital as the differentiators.

### Gaps
- I did not open the original Mauboussin & Callahan paper. The 10% to 6% figures rest on secondary summaries. The "6-sigma to hit .400 today" claim is unverified.

## 3. The behavior gap: investor returns vs fund returns, the disposition effect, and overtrading

### Takeaway
Investors consistently earn less than the funds they own, because of timing, chasing past performance, and overtrading. The gap runs from about 1 point a year in the U.S. (Morningstar) to 5+ points in Indian equity funds (Axis). It widens with volatility: small caps, sector and thematic funds, and leverage. Frequent traders lose about 6–7 points a year mostly to costs, not to bad picks.

### Cited Findings
**Morningstar "Mind the Gap" (US 2025 edition, 10 years to 31 Dec 2025)**
- Dollar-weighted investor return of about 8.7% a year vs total fund return of about 9.9%, a gap of about 1.2 points a year, roughly 12% of the return. — [Morningstar](https://www.morningstar.com/business/insights/research/mind-the-gap-2025)
- Least-volatile funds had a gap of about −0.4%, the most volatile about −2.1%. The gap is wider for ETFs than for open-end funds, narrowest for allocation and target-date funds, and active vs passive is *not* the main driver. — [Morningstar](https://www.morningstar.com/business/insights/research/mind-the-gap-2025); [Advisor Perspectives 2026](https://www.advisorperspectives.com/commentaries/2026/08/21/what-advisors-can-learn-from-the-investor-return-gap)

**DALBAR QAIB**
- 2025 QAIB (calendar 2024): average equity investor +16.54% vs S&P 500 +25.02%, an 848 basis-point gap, the second largest in a decade. — [DALBAR 2025 press release](https://www.dalbar.com/Portals/dalbar/Cache/News/PressReleases/2025QAIBPressRelease.pdf)
- 2026 QAIB (calendar 2025): investor 17.16% vs S&P 500 17.88%, a 0.72-point gap, the smallest since 2012. — [DALBAR 2026 press release](https://www.dalbar.com/PressReleases/doc/2026QAIBReportPressRelease.pdf)
- **Caveat:** DALBAR's methodology has been criticised by academics and by Morningstar, so Morningstar's dollar-weighted figures are the more conservative source. The size of the DALBAR gap varies wildly from year to year.

**India**
- Axis MF, 2003–2022: equity and hybrid funds returned about 19.1% a year, the average investor earned about 13.8%, and a hypothetical SIP earned about 15.2%. Axis is a fund house and has a commercial interest. — [Axis MF "Thinking Aloud"](https://www.axismf.com/cms/sites/default/files/pdf-factsheets/Thinking%20Aloud%20-%20Using%20your%20Mutual%20Funds%20More%20Effectively.pdf)
- Value Research studied 170 diversified equity funds over 10 years, using actual investor cash flows. The gap ranged from 1% to about 10%. Reported via Mint; I did not open the original. — [Mint via Magzter](https://www.magzter.com/en/stories/newspaper/Mint-New-Delhi/WHY-DO-YOUR-RETURNS-RARELY-MATCH-WHAT-FUNDS-DELIVER)
- Morningstar India example: a top fund returned 40% over 3 years, but the average investor earned 20%, because about 75% of the inflows arrived in the final year. — [Morningstar India](https://www.morningstar.in/posts/69884/why-does-the-funds-return-differ-from-yours.aspx)
- Advisorkhoj: investor returns trailed both point-to-point and SIP returns in equity, hybrid, and debt funds. Stopping SIPs during corrections defeats their purpose. — [Advisorkhoj](https://advisorkhoj.com/news/Mutual-Fund/MFs%20Investor%20Returns%20vs%20Fund%20Returns)

**Academic: overtrading and the disposition effect**
- Barber & Odean (2000), "Trading Is Hazardous to Your Wealth": 66,465 households at a discount broker, 1991–96. The top-turnover quintile averaged more than 250% annual turnover and netted 11.4% a year, against 18.5% for the least active. Gross returns were similar, so costs (about 3% commission plus 1% spread per round trip) explain the gap. — [Odean (Berkeley) review paper](https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/odean/Papers%20current%20versions/Individual_Investor_Performance_Final.pdf); [NBER w16022](https://www.nber.org/papers/w16022.pdf). Critics point to the pre-online-trading data and rational adjustment-cost explanations. — [SEC comment letter](https://sec.gov/comments/s7-12-23/s71223-281699-688282.pdf)
- Odean (1998), "Are Investors Reluctant to Realize Their Losses?", 10,000 discount-brokerage accounts: investors realize gains more readily than losses. The winners they sell go on to *outperform* the losers they keep, so the behavior lowers returns, especially in taxable accounts. — [Odean, Berkeley](https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/odean/papers/disposition/disposition.html); [CFA digest](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/research/cfa-digest/1999/05/are-investors-reluctant-to-realize-their-losses-digest-summary). (The "3.4% excess return" figure from my query was **not** confirmed in the sources I accessed.)
- Grinblatt, Keloharju & Linnainmaa (JFE 2012), Finnish IQ data: high-IQ investors show less disposition effect, do more tax-loss selling, have better timing, picking, and execution, and pay lower trading costs. Intelligence helps, but the authors note the direction of causality is hard to establish. — [NBER conference paper](https://conference.nber.org/confer/2009/BEF09/Grinblatt_Keloharju_Linnainmaa.pdf); [RePEc](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jfinec:v:104:y:2012:i:2:p:339-362)

### Inferences
- Even a modest yearly gap is costly. [CALC] Over 30 years, 9.9% compounds to about 17x and 8.7% to about 12x, roughly 30% less ending wealth. Indian gaps of 5 points (19.1% vs 13.8%) compound to about a 4x difference in ending wealth over 30 years.
- The gap is largest where the "exciting" opportunities are (volatile funds, small caps, F&O). People who know the principles tend to move into those areas, and the behavior cost is highest there.

### Gaps
- I found no independent academic study of the Indian investor-vs-fund-return gap. AMFI publishes SIP stoppage and continuation ratios, but I did not retrieve them.

## 4. Temperament: holding through drawdowns, the "dead investors" claim, and sitting still

### Takeaway
Nearly every great long-term stock suffered drawdowns of 50–95% on the way up. Knowing to "hold winners" is easy. Holding through a 90% fall, which needs a 9x rebound to get back to even, is what separates outcomes. The popular Fidelity "best investors were dead or forgot" study is a **MYTH**: no such study has been found.

### Cited Findings
- Amazon fell about 94% (some sources say 95%) from December 1999 to late 2001, from roughly $113 to $6, and took about a decade to regain the peak. Long-term holders still got rich. — [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2018/12/18/dotcom-bubble-amazon-stock-lost-more-than-90percent-long-term-investors-still-got-rich.html); [Benzinga (Bezos: "the stock is not the company")](https://benzinga.com/tech/25/05/45426247/jeff-bezos-once-watched-amazons-stock-collapse-from-113-to-6-but-held-on-to-the-belief-that-the-stock-is-not-the-company-heres-how-he-weathered-that-st)
- Apple, Microsoft, and Nvidia each had drawdowns of more than 80%, and most stocks fall more than 50% at some point. — [Irish Times](https://www.irishtimes.com/your-money/2025/06/17/the-agony-and-the-equity-most-stocks-fall-more-than-50-at-some-point). Netflix fell 60% or more four times, twice by about 90%. — [A Wealth of Common Sense](https://awealthofcommonsense.com/2021/03/owning-the-best-stocks-is-hard/)
- Mauboussin (Consilient Observer, "Drawdowns and Recoveries"): the top wealth creators (Apple, Microsoft, NVIDIA, Alphabet, Amazon, ExxonMobil) added $17.1tn, but buying and holding them meant enduring large drawdowns. — [Eaton Vance / Mauboussin PDF](https://www.eatonvance.com/content/dam/im/assets/publication/thought-leadership/consilient-observer/article_drawdownsandrecoveries.pdf)
- Dot-com tech IPOs had a median terminal drawdown of −85%, and the survivors produced some of history's highest returns. This is survivorship again: most never recovered. — [Lambda Finance](https://www.lambdafin.com/articles/tech-ipo-drawdown-history)
- **MYTH: the Fidelity "dead or forgot" study.** It traces to James O'Shaughnessy recounting it on Barry Ritholtz's Bloomberg radio show. No original Fidelity research has been located, and commentators call it "folklore". — [Wood Gundy (CIBC)](https://woodgundyadvisors.cibc.com/david-ricciardelli/blog/3073570-Yes-the-investors-with-the-best-returns-were-dead/); [Dividend Growth Investor](https://dividendgrowthinvestor.com/2021/01/the-best-investors-in-world-are-dead.html); [Motley Fool discussion](https://discussion.fool.com/t/best-investing-strategy/34550). The lesson it is meant to illustrate is supported by real data: Barber & Odean and Morningstar, in Section 3.

### Inferences
- [CALC] Recovery math: a −50% fall needs +100% to break even, −75% needs +300%, −90% needs +900%, and −94% (Amazon) needs about +1,567%. Leverage turns any of these drawdowns into permanent ruin. This is the link to Archegos and LTCM.
- Survivorship applies to temperament too. "Hold through a −90% drawdown" is good advice only for the Amazons. For the median dot-com IPO, it meant riding the stock to −85% and staying there. Holding needs conviction grounded in knowledge of the business, the Bezos "stock is not the company" view. Without that, holding is just stubbornness. The deciding variable is domain expertise, not a rule.

### Gaps
- I found no rigorous study directly measuring "ability to sit still" or ego as a predictor of investor outcomes, beyond the turnover and disposition research above.
- I did not retrieve Phelps ("100 to 1 in the Stock Market") or Mayer ("100 Baggers") drawdown data.
- I did not retrieve drawdown histories for Indian multibaggers such as Eicher or Bajaj Finance.

## 5. Compounding and time: Buffett's late wealth, return-rate vs duration math, capital base

### Takeaway
Time and starting capital dominate the rate of return. About 99% of Buffett's fortune came after age 65. High returns for a few years build very little. ₹1 lakh compounding at 30% for a decade is about ₹14 lakh, which is not generational wealth. Ordinary people who got rich through markets (Ronald Read, Grace Groner) did it through decades of patience, frugality, and reinvested dividends, not brilliance.

### Cited Findings
- Housel: about 99% of Buffett's net worth was accumulated after age 65. His book cited $81.5bn earned after 65 and $84.2bn after 50. — [CNBC 2024](https://www.cnbc.com/2024/05/03/most-of-warren-buffetts-wealth-came-after-age-65-heres-why.html); [CSQ / Housel](https://csq.com/2020/08/how-warren-buffet-amassed-his-fortune/). Creative Planning (2025) puts it at about 98% after 65. — [Benzinga](https://feeds2.benzinga.com/general/market-summary/25/05/45399940/warren-buffett-shows-how-patience-pays-98-of-his-160-billion-wealth-came-after-turning-65-)
- Housel's counterfactual: if Buffett had started at 30 with $25,000, earned 22% a year, and stopped at 60, he would have about $11.9m, roughly 99.9% less than his actual fortune. — [CSQ / Housel](https://csq.com/2020/08/how-warren-buffet-amassed-his-fortune/); [Irish Times 2025](https://www.irishtimes.com/your-money/2025/11/15/going-quiet-at-95-buffett-shows-why-time-beats-returns)
- Ronald Read: a Vermont gas-station attendant and JCPenney janitor who died in 2014 at 92 with about $8m. He held dividend stocks in companies he understood and left about $6m to the local hospital and library (the split varies by source). — [CNBC 2016](https://www.cnbc.com/amp/2016/08/29/janitor-secretly-amassed-an-8-million-fortune.html); [AJC](https://www.ajc.com/news/weird-news/who-was-ronald-read-the-janitor-who-amassed-million-fortune-and-gave-all-away/unEhOIxXLh7f66bOtHyFbI)
- Grace Groner: an Abbott Labs secretary who bought 3 shares for $180 in 1935, reinvested the dividends, and died in 2010 at 100 with about $7.2m, which went to Lake Forest College. — [Business Insider NL](https://businessinsider.nl/a-secretary-turned-180-into-7-2-million-by-holding-her-employers-stock-for-75-years/); [Wikipedia: Grace Groner](https://en.Wikipedia.com/wiki/Grace_Groner). Note the survivorship: she held a single employer stock that happened to compound. Many single-stock employee holders (Enron and others) were wiped out.

### Inferences [CALC, my own arithmetic]
- 20% a year for 30 years gives 237x. 50% a year for 3 years gives only 3.4x. Duration beats intensity.
- 12% for 30 years gives about 30x, 22% for 30 years about 390x, and 30% for 30 years about 2,620x.
- ₹1 lakh at 30% grows to:
  - about ₹13.8 lakh after 10 years
  - about ₹1.9 crore after 20 years
  - about ₹26 crore after 30 years
- Even an elite rate of 30% sustained for 30 years (a record almost no one has achieved) turns ₹1 lakh into about ₹26 crore. That is wealthy, but not on a billionaire scale.
- Capital base and time are the levers. Founders win because their ownership stake is a very large multiple of their savings: equity in a company they built, not cash they invested.
- Longevity is a hidden input. Buffett's result depended on investing from about age 11 to his 90s. Read and Groner both lived past 90.

### Gaps
- I found no Indian equivalent of Read/Groner with documented sources.

## 6. What the ultra-successful say the secret is, and what research says predicts success

### Takeaway
Successful investors consistently name survival (avoid ruin and leverage), patience and time, independent thinking, staying in their circle of competence, and owner or operator-level knowledge. Research partially supports this. Low turnover, low costs, avoiding the disposition effect, and (in Finnish data) higher IQ predict better retail results. Being a founder or operator is the dominant route to extreme wealth. No study shows that knowing principles predicts outcomes.

### Cited Findings
- Avoiding ruin: Buffett on LTCM ("risked what they did have and did need"). — [Novel Investor](https://novelinvestor.com/buffetts-lessons-long-term-capital-management/). Buffett also says you don't need a 160 IQ: temperament matters more than intellect. — [Value Research](https://www.valueresearchonline.com/stories/225179/buffett-right-dont-need-160-iq-good-investor/)
- Patience and time: Housel's Buffett analysis, in Section 5. — [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2024/05/03/most-of-warren-buffetts-wealth-came-after-age-65-heres-why.html)

### Inferences
- The "missing something" is a set of non-knowledge factors that interact: (a) time and starting capital, (b) temperament under drawdowns, (c) no leverage or ruin exposure, (d) an information edge from operating in the domain, which justifies holding concentrated positions, and (e) luck, which dominates any record shorter than a decade. Only (d) is knowledge, and it is domain knowledge, not knowledge of investing principles.
- Concentrated founders and concentrated speculators look alike before the outcome, and only the survivors become role models. That is the core survivorship trap behind "learn the principles and get rich".

### Gaps
- I found no large-sample study showing that financial literacy or knowledge of investing principles predicts portfolio returns. The literacy literature mostly links it to participation and diversification. I did not research this in depth.
- I did not retrieve or verify individual quotes from Munger, Jhunjhunwala, or Damani.
