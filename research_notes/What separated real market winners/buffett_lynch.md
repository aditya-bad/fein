# What actually made young Warren Buffett and Peter Lynch successful: the concrete, unglamorous specifics

Researcher notes, compiled 2026-11-19. Verification labels used below:
- **[VERIFIED-PRIMARY]**: read in a primary source this session (the paper itself, the interview transcript, or Buffett's own words as reported by a primary outlet).
- **[SECONDARY]**: from credible secondary write-ups; plausible but not checked against the primary source.
- **[PRIMARY-NOT-REFETCHED]**: a well-known passage in a Berkshire letter. The URL is the primary document, but I did not re-open it this session, so the writer should spot-check the wording.
- **[MYTH/DISPUTED]**: widely repeated, but the evidence is weak or contradicts it.

## Buffett: early career, specific trades, edge, capital/leverage structure, workload, mistakes

### Takeaway
Young Buffett's edge was huge amounts of reading (whole manuals of tiny, ignored securities), applied to very small sums where obscure net-nets and special situations were available, under a fee structure with no management fee. Later Berkshire's edge changed into roughly 1.6–1.7x leverage at below-T-bill cost (insurance float plus AAA debt), applied to cheap, safe, high-quality stocks and held for decades. Most of his dollar wealth came from that late compounding, not from the famous early trades.

### Cited Findings

**Early capital and work ethic (pre-1956)**
- Delivered newspapers, sold refurbished golf balls and stamp sets, and ran pinball machines in barbershops. From all his ventures combined he had saved about **$9,800** by around the time he finished school [SECONDARY]. I found no source confirming the exact age or the "Washington Post route" detail — [Frederik Journals, "The Hustler"](https://www.frederikjournals.com/p/the-hustler-lessons-from-a-young); [Saber Capital, Buffett's Early Investments](https://sabercapitalmgt.com/buffetts-early-investments/)
- **Moody's manuals:** Buffett reportedly went through about 20,000 pages of the Moody's industrial, transportation, bank and finance manuals, twice. In early 1952, while flipping through the Moody's Bank & Finance manual at his Omaha brokerage job, he found **Western Insurance Securities**. He recalled its "price range in Moody's financial manual was $12 to $20" while it earned about $24/share the prior year, so it traded at roughly 1x earnings [SECONDARY] — [findvalue23, Western Insurance](https://findvalue23.substack.com/p/history-warren-buffett-and-western); [GuruFocus, Western Insurance](https://www.gurufocus.com/news/1149512/some-thoughts-on-warren-buffetts-early-investments-in-western-insurance-services)
- **GEICO 1951:** about 65% of his net worth (about $10,282) went into GEICO in 1951. He sold in 1952 for $15,259 and rotated into cheaper stocks such as Western Insurance [SECONDARY]. He later called selling GEICO a costly error — [GuruFocus "How Buffett Crushed the Dow in 1951"](https://www.gurufocus.com/news/248721/how-buffett-crushed-the-dow-in-1951-lessons-for-investing-with-small-sums); [Fool](https://www.fool.com/investing/2019/12/29/heres-how-much-money-warren-buffett-has-made-in-ge.aspx)
- **1951 personal return:** about **75.8% vs. the Dow's 21.3%** [SECONDARY]. A circulating "144%" figure for 1951 has no source I could find [MYTH/DISPUTED] — [GuruFocus](https://www.gurufocus.com/news/248721/how-buffett-crushed-the-dow-in-1951-lessons-for-investing-with-small-sums)
- **Graham-Newman apprenticeship, 1954–56:** starting salary **$12,000/yr**. The sources agree on these dates; one outlier says 1951 [SECONDARY] — [Financial History magazine](https://fhmagazine.org/financial-history-134-summer-2020/0850549001596540706/p15); [dividendgrowthinvestor](https://dividendgrowthinvestor.com/2020/08/warren-buffett-americas-youngest-early.html)

**Buffett Partnership Ltd. (1956/57–1969)**
- Started 1956 with **$105,100** from family and close friends [SECONDARY, from the letters] — [Rational Walk, Highlights from BPL letters](https://rationalwalk.com/highlights-from-buffett-partnership-letters/)
- **Fees:** no management fee. He took 25% of profits above a 6% annual hurdle, with deficits carried forward. That is not "2 and 20," and the general partner got no guaranteed payment — [Rational Walk](https://rationalwalk.com/highlights-from-buffett-partnership-letters/); [Hedge Fund Alpha, Munger on BPL fees](https://hedgefundalpha.com/strategies/charlie-mungers-commentary-on-the-1960s-buffett-partnerships-fee-structure/)
- **Returns:** limited partners compounded about **23.8%/yr net vs. 7.4% for the Dow**. Gross is often cited around 29.5% [SECONDARY]. LPs trailed the Dow in only one year, 1958 — [Rational Walk](https://rationalwalk.com/highlights-from-buffett-partnership-letters/); [Picture Perfect Portfolios](https://pictureperfectportfolios.com/warren-buffett-partnership-years-before-berkshire/) (lower-quality aggregator; net figures vary from 23.6% to 23.8%)
- **Why he closed it (1969):** "The only way to slow down is to stop." He did not want to be "totally occupied with out-pacing an investment rabbit all my life." He also judged the late-1960s market short of bargains. Partners could take Berkshire stock in the final distribution — [Rational Walk](https://rationalwalk.com/highlights-from-buffett-partnership-letters/)

**Specific cigar-butt and special-situation trades**
- **Sanborn Map (1958–60):** about **25% of partnership assets** went into Sanborn in late 1958. The map business's after-tax profits had fallen from over $500,000/yr in the late 1930s to under $100,000 in 1958–59, but the company's investment portfolio was worth more than its share price. Buffett got a board seat and forced the value out [SECONDARY, from the 1960 letter] — [GuruFocus 1960 letter](https://www.gurufocus.com/news/7227/warren-buffett-partnership-letter-of-1960); [MOI Global / Ground Rules](https://moiglobal.com/warren-buffetts-ground-rules/)
- **Dempster Mill (1956–63):** built gradually from an undervalued-security position to control. The partnership owned 70% and associates another 10%. At end-1961, book value was about $75/share and Buffett carried it at $35. He brought in Harry Bottle to liquidate inventory and turn the business around, and the operating assets were sold in 1963. The deal caused local backlash in Beatrice, Nebraska — [MOI Global](https://moiglobal.com/warren-buffetts-ground-rules/); [Hedge Fund Alpha, Dempster](https://hedgefundalpha.com/strategies/warren-buffett-charlie-munger-dempster-mill/)
- **Rockwood & Co. (cocoa arbitrage, 1954, Graham-Newman era):** Jay Pritzker's Rockwood offered $36 of cocoa beans per share while the shares traded at $34. Buffett bought 222 shares himself, and per Miller he skipped the arbitrage and held. The stock reached about $85, for roughly $13,000 of gain vs. about $444 from the arb [SECONDARY, single source; paywalled] — [MOI Global](https://moiglobal.com/warren-buffetts-ground-rules/)
- **American Express, salad-oil scandal (1963–64):** Buffett checked whether customers still used Amex cards and traveler's checks at Ross's Steak House and other Omaha businesses, and through a broker friend, Henry Brandt, polled bank tellers, restaurants and cardholders. He found usage unaffected. Accounts say the partnership built a stake of about **$13M, about 40% of partnership assets**, and exited around 1968 for about $33M. Purchase price is reported variously at $35–41 [SECONDARY; the 40% figure comes from a few sources and I did not verify it against the BPL letters]. Buffett's 1965 letter is often paraphrased as saying he would go to 40% of net worth in a single security under conditions of extremely high probability; I could not fetch the primary text — [The Grain Desk](https://thegraindesk.substack.com/p/main-street-still-trusted-the-card); [The Value Investor](https://www.thevalueinvestor.org/p/warren-buffett-s-investment-in-american-express-a-case-study-in-conviction-investing); [Fortune 2012](https://fortune.com/2012/11/21/why-warren-buffetts-betting-big-on-american-express/amp)

**Small-capital advantage (Buffett's own claims)**
- 1999 BusinessWeek: "I think I could make you 50% a year on $1 million… it's a huge structural advantage not to have a lot of money" [widely reported, attributed to BusinessWeek 1999] — [Yahoo/Moneywise roundup](https://www.aol.com/finance/warren-buffett-certain-could-earn-121300309.html); [Capitalmind 2014](https://premium.capitalmind.in/2014/03/buffett-got-the-answer-right-in-1999-but-the-reasoning-didnt-work-out/)
- **Korea (2004–05):** Buffett said he bought about 20 Korean stocks with personal money by leafing through a **Citigroup-provided reference book** compiled in Korea, with one page per listed company (WSJ 2005, as quoted). One example was **Daehan Flour Mills**, with about 25% of the Korean wheat-flour market, EPS 18,874 won, book value 200,000 won, about 100,000 won of cash and securities per share, and a price of 38,000 won, i.e. about 2x earnings (Columbia student-visit account). A blog transcription gives EPS of about 18,000 won instead — [The Buffett Blog, quoting WSJ](https://thebuffettblog.blogspot.com/2005/11/two-from-journal-confirmation-on-korea.html); [Columbia Business School PDF](https://drupalgsb-test.paas.cc.columbia.edu/sites/default/files-efs/pubfiles/3514/International%20Investing%20with%20the%20Oracle%20of%20Omaha.pdf); [Korea Times 2007](https://www.koreatimes.co.kr/business/companies/20071025/buffett-sees-seoul-market-attractive)
  - Correction to the brief: the Korea episode is **2004–05, not 1999**. The 1999 item is the "50% on $1M" quote.
- In the 2014 Berkshire letter, Buffett wrote that his cigar-butt approach "worked very well while I was managing small sums" but became far less effective at scale. Under Munger's influence he shifted to "buy wonderful businesses at fair prices" [PRIMARY-NOT-REFETCHED] — [Berkshire 2014 letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/2014ltr.pdf)
- **See's Candies (1972):** bought for $25M, when it had about $30M of sales and under $5M of pretax earnings. It then produced large cumulative pretax earnings needing little reinvested capital, which is Buffett's model "dream business." Munger pushed him to pay up for quality [PRIMARY-NOT-REFETCHED; see the 2007 letter's See's discussion] — [Berkshire 2007 letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/2007ltr.pdf)

**Leverage and structural edge: "Buffett's Alpha" (Frazzini, Kabiller, Pedersen; FAJ 2018, vol. 74 no. 4)** [VERIFIED-PRIMARY, read from the published PDF]
- From October 1976 to March 2017, $1 in Berkshire grew to more than **$3,685**. The average excess return over T-bills was **18.6%** vs. the market's 7.5%, at **23.5% volatility** (market 15.3%). The **Sharpe ratio was 0.79** vs. the market's 0.49, beta 0.69, and information ratio 0.64. Berkshire had the highest Sharpe ratio of any US stock or mutual fund with a 40-plus-year history — [Buffett's Alpha PDF](https://gwern.net/doc/economics/2018-frazzini.pdf); [CFA Institute listing](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/research/financial-analysts-journal/2018/ip-v3-n1-13-demystifying-buffetts-investment-success)
- **Leverage was about 1.7-to-1** (total assets to equity). Counting only equity, debt and float, it was about 1.4-to-1. The 2012 working-paper draft said about 1.6 — [PDF](https://gwern.net/doc/economics/2018-frazzini.pdf); [GuruFocus on the 2012 draft](https://www.gurufocus.com/news/614298/is-the-secret-to-buffetts-returns-leverage-)
- The average cost of insurance float was **1.72%/yr, about 3 percentage points below the T-bill rate**. Float was **about 35% of liabilities**. Debt was rated AAA from 1989 to 2009, and in 2002 Berkshire issued the first-ever negative-coupon security. Berkshire also financed capital spending with deferred taxes from accelerated depreciation ($28B in 2011) — [PDF](https://gwern.net/doc/economics/2018-frazzini.pdf)
- Leverage alone does not explain the record. 1.7x leverage on the market gives about a 12.7% excess return, well short of 18.6% — [PDF](https://gwern.net/doc/economics/2018-frazzini.pdf)
- Alpha became **statistically insignificant once betting-against-beta (BAB) and quality-minus-junk (QMJ) were controlled for**. For example, the public-stock portfolio's alpha fell to an insignificant 0.3%. A systematic "Buffett-style" portfolio of cheap, safe, quality stocks with matched leverage performed comparably — [PDF](https://gwern.net/doc/economics/2018-frazzini.pdf)
- **Public stocks did better than the wholly-owned private businesses**, which suggests his returns come more from stock selection than from his effect on management — [PDF](https://gwern.net/doc/economics/2018-frazzini.pdf)
- The authors' conclusion: "he stuck to a good strategy… for a long time period, surviving rough periods where others might have been forced into a fire sale or a career shift, and he boosted his returns by using leverage." His genius lay in "recognizing early on that these investment themes work, applying leverage without ever having a fire sale, and sticking to his principles" — [PDF](https://gwern.net/doc/economics/2018-frazzini.pdf)
- A caveat from commentary: cost-free float is not typical for the P&C industry, where premiums often fall short of claims and expenses. Berkshire's underwriting discipline made the float cheap — [GuruFocus on float](https://www.gurufocus.com/news/981189/Some-Insight-Into-How-Buffett-Has-Used-Float-to-Leverage-His-Returns-)

**Late-life compounding**
- "About 99% of Buffett's wealth came after his 50th birthday" is the most common version. Morgan Housel has used **60th** (book and podcast) and reportedly **65th** (CNBC 2022). The age varies by telling, but the point holds: most of his dollar wealth came from late compounding [SECONDARY] — [Money.com](https://money.com/warren-buffett-retirement-saving-after-50/); [Housel transcript, singjupost](https://singjupost.com/transcript-morgan-housel-what-you-need-to-master-and-avoid-to-get-rich-stay-rich-and-build-wealth/); [GOBankingRates](https://www.gobankingrates.com/?p=2260069)

**Mistakes**
- **Berkshire textile (1962–65):** the partnership bought stock as a cigar butt. Seabury Stanton agreed to buy Buffett's shares back at $11.50, then sent a written tender at $11 3/8. Buffett bought control instead and fired Stanton. In 2010 he told CNBC it was the "dumbest" stock he ever bought and a "$200 billion" mistake. That is a counterfactual: Berkshire would be worth about twice as much had he put the capital into an insurance company — [CNBC transcript](https://www.cnbc.com/id/39724884); [CNBC article 2010](https://www.cnbc.com/2010/10/18/warren-buffett-buying-berkshire-hathaway-was-200-billion-blunder.html)
- **Dexter Shoe (1993):** paid **$433M in Berkshire stock**. 2007 letter: "To date, Dexter is the worst deal that I've made… I gave away 1.6% of a wonderful business." The moat vanished as imports took about 93% of the US shoe market by 1999. The forgone Berkshire shares have been valued at about $8.7B to $14B+ depending on date — [Business Insider](https://www.businessinsider.nl/warren-buffetts-most-gruesome-mistake-was-buying-dexter-shoe-with-berkshire-hathaway-stock-heres-a-look-back-at-his-12-billion-error); [CNBC 2023](https://www.cnbc.com/2023/03/02/warren-buffetts-investing-mistakes.html)
- **Airlines (2020):** Berkshire added to its airline holdings in February 2020, then sold all four major US airlines in April. At the May 2, 2020 meeting he said "the world has changed" and called it "my mistake" — [Kiplinger](https://www.kiplinger.com/article/investing/t052-c008-s001-buffett-dumps-airline-stocks-cheerleads-america.html); [Investing.com/Reuters](https://investing.com/news/stock-market-news/berkshire-sells-entire-stakes-in-us-airlines-buffett-2158833); [Fool 2021](https://www.fool.com/investing/2021/05/21/warren-buffetts-latest-investing-mistakes)
- **IBM (2011–2018):** I found no source this session. See Gaps.

### Inferences
- **Replicable:** exhaustive reading of primary sources (manuals, filings), concentrated bets in small and obscure securities, field checks of customer behavior (the Amex steakhouse test), a 1x-earnings or below-net-current-asset discipline, and fee and incentive alignment. These worked because his capital was tiny (under $10k up to a few $M) and the universe was unscreened before computers.
- **Not replicable or circumstantial:** mentorship by Graham and a seat inside Graham-Newman's deal flow. The 1950s market was full of net-nets that screens now find in seconds. Later, permanent capital at about 1.7x leverage costing below T-bills, with no redemptions and no margin calls, cannot be had by an individual using broker margin.
- **The biggest underrated factor is longevity plus never being forced to sell.** The academic evidence says his Sharpe ratio (0.79) was "very good but not superhuman." The extreme wealth came from about 60 years of compounding, much of it after age 50–65.
- The famous early trades (Sanborn, Dempster, Amex) built the reputation and the first several $M. In dollar terms they are tiny next to the float-funded Berkshire decades.
- Buffett's mistakes cluster in **businesses with a fading moat bought on price** (textiles, Dexter, airlines). He usually compounded them by paying in Berkshire stock. That is the cigar-butt habit failing at scale.

### Gaps

- IBM: I found no source this session. My background knowledge is that Berkshire bought about $10B+ starting in 2011, Buffett said at the 2017 meeting he had valued it wrong, and the stake was exited by 2018 around breakeven. **Do not use this without a citation.**

## Peter Lynch: Magellan 1977–1990, workload, holdings, tenbaggers, investor returns, retirement

### Takeaway
Lynch's edge was industrial-scale legwork: hundreds of company visits and hundreds of annual reports a year, 13-hour days, Saturdays, a week a month on the road, with very high early turnover and extreme diversification once the fund grew. Underneath was a small starting base ($18–22M) and a then-unusual Fidelity freedom to visit any company. The "average Magellan investor lost money" claim is an unsourced myth. The accurate version is that investors' dollar-weighted returns lagged the fund's time-weighted return, roughly 13–17% vs. 22–29%, depending on period and method.

### Cited Findings

**Background**
- He caddied from age 11 at Brae Burn Country Club and caddied for Fidelity's president, D. George Sullivan. In college he bought **Flying Tiger** air freight with about $1,000 (around $7/share) on the idea that "air cargo was going to be a thing of the future," and it was his first tenbagger — [PBS Frontline interview](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/pages/frontline/shows/betting/pros/lynch.html) [VERIFIED-PRIMARY]; [CapexCat](https://capexcat.substack.com/p/peter-lynch-buy-what-you-know) [SECONDARY]
- He was a Fidelity summer intern in 1966 (about 75 applicants for 3 spots), an analyst from 1969 at $16,000/yr, and director of research in 1974 at age 30 — [CapexCat](https://capexcat.substack.com/p/peter-lynch-buy-what-you-know) [SECONDARY]

**Fund size and performance**
- "When I took over in May of 1977, the fund was $20 million… When I left Magellan Fund, it was 14 billion" — [PBS Frontline](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/pages/frontline/shows/betting/pros/lynch.html) [VERIFIED-PRIMARY]. CRSP shows $22M at end-1976, and Lynch's own count elsewhere is about $18M. Assets were $107M at end-1981 and passed $1B in April 1983. The fund was closed to new investors until it opened to the public in 1981, when it had about $100M — [CapexCat](https://capexcat.substack.com/p/peter-lynch-buy-what-you-know); [CBS MoneyWatch](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/lessons-from-a-great-fund-managers-record)
- **Returns:** 29.2%/yr vs. the market's 15.5% (CRSP, June 1977–May 1990). It beat the market in 108 of 156 months, with about 7.9%/yr alpha — [CapexCat](https://capexcat.substack.com/p/peter-lynch-buy-what-you-know). CBS gives **26.4%/yr vs. the S&P 500's 13.3%** for 1977–1990. The sources differ by method and period — [CBS MoneyWatch 2010](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/lessons-from-a-great-fund-managers-record); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Lynch)
- **The small early base mattered:** the best years came while the fund was under $100M and closed to new money. It only opened to the public in 1981 at about $100M. In 1986 alone it took in $2.3B of new money on a $4.1B base — [CBS MoneyWatch](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/lessons-from-a-great-fund-managers-record)

**Turnover and diversification**
- First-year turnover was **343%**, then about 300%/yr for the next three years [SECONDARY] — [CapexCat](https://capexcat.substack.com/p/peter-lynch-buy-what-you-know); [Diversified Chaos](https://diversifiedchaos.substack.com/p/the-relentless-diversifier-how-peter)
- Holdings fell to about 90 stocks during heavy redemptions and grew to **about 1,400 by 1989**. He bought more than 15,000 different stocks over 13 years by his own count. He said "I owned thousands of stocks" — [PBS Frontline](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/pages/frontline/shows/betting/pros/lynch.html) [VERIFIED-PRIMARY]; [CapexCat](https://capexcat.substack.com/p/peter-lynch-buy-what-you-know); [Deseret News 1990](https://www.deseret.com/1990/1/7/18841992/magellan-fund-manager-hopes-to-keep-the-edge-in-90s)

**Workload and edge**
- "I was working six days a week and that wasn't even enough." "I could see any company I wanted to see… I didn't have to get permission to go visit companies in California or Indiana" — [PBS Frontline](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/pages/frontline/shows/betting/pros/lynch.html) [VERIFIED-PRIMARY]
- He arrived around 6:45 a.m. and stayed about 13 hours, worked about 6-hour Saturdays, spent a week a month on the road, made **200+ company visits and read about 700 annual reports a year** (*Beating the Street*), and saw up to 3 companies a day [SECONDARY, citing Time and his books] — [CapexCat](https://capexcat.substack.com/p/peter-lynch-buy-what-you-know)

**Specific big winners**
- **Chrysler:** he began buying around $6/share. By end-July 1982 it was **5% of Magellan**, the limit for a diversified fund, and it was the largest holding that fall [SECONDARY, single source]. Lynch: "Chrysler was my biggest position." People asked him "Don't you know they're going bankrupt?" — [PBS Frontline](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/pages/frontline/shows/betting/pros/lynch.html) [VERIFIED-PRIMARY]; [CapexCat](https://capexcat.substack.com/p/peter-lynch-buy-what-you-know)
- **Fannie Mae:** bought in 1977 and re-bought in 1982, 1984, 1987 and 1988. Reported as the biggest dollar winner at about $500M of gains [SECONDARY, single source] — [CapexCat](https://capexcat.substack.com/p/peter-lynch-buy-what-you-know); [Diversified Chaos](https://diversifiedchaos.substack.com/p/the-relentless-diversifier-how-peter)
- **Taco Bell** was Magellan's largest position in 1978, before PepsiCo bought it at $42. **La Quinta** rose about elevenfold after a 1978 purchase. **Hanes** came from his wife's tip about L'eggs pantyhose. **Stop & Shop** went $6 to $60 in under 3 years. Dunkin' Donuts, The Gap and Service Corp. are cited as examples [SECONDARY] — [CapexCat](https://capexcat.substack.com/p/peter-lynch-buy-what-you-know)
- "I think I listed over a hundred stocks that went up over ten-fold when I ran Magellan." He also credited luck: "it got really lucky because it went up for another reason" — [PBS Frontline](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/pages/frontline/shows/betting/pros/lynch.html) [VERIFIED-PRIMARY]
- On timing: "I don't remember anybody predicting the market right more than once" — [PBS Frontline](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/pages/frontline/shows/betting/pros/lynch.html)

**"The average Magellan investor lost money": verdict [MYTH/DISPUTED]**
- A 1996 *Globe and Mail* column found that a Fidelity spokesman **denied that Fidelity ever did such a study or that Lynch ever said it**. Ibbotson also denied producing one. The only firm that had studied Magellan, Micropal, found the fund lost money in 17% of rolling 12-month periods. The columnist's verdict on "half lost money": "Not likely" — [Bylo.org reprint, "Magellan Myth"](https://bylo.org/magellan.html)
- Fidelity Canada's own article hedges: "we don't know how many investors actually lost money… or so the story goes" — [Fidelity.ca](https://fidelity.ca/en/insights/articles/how-investors-lost-money)
- What is supported is a timing gap. CBS MoneyWatch's dollar-weighted estimate for 1981–1990 was **13.4%/yr for the average investor vs. the fund's 21.8%** (S&P 16.2%). The method is not documented beyond "adjusting for timing and size of cash flows." A separate CRSP-based estimate gives investor dollar-weighted returns of about 17.4%/yr over Lynch's tenure — [CBS MoneyWatch](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/lessons-from-a-great-fund-managers-record); [CapexCat](https://capexcat.substack.com/p/peter-lynch-buy-what-you-know)

**Retirement at 46 (May 31, 1990)**
- "The pressure wasn't it. It was just too much time" — [PBS Frontline](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/pages/frontline/shows/betting/pros/lynch.html) [VERIFIED-PRIMARY]
- His father died at 46, and Lynch turned 46 in January 1990. His daughters were 15, 11 and 7. He said he had "two gears, overdrive and park" (Time, April 1990) — [CapexCat](https://capexcat.substack.com/p/peter-lynch-buy-what-you-know)
- "Buy what you know" is a distortion. In 2023 he said "I did not say invest in the stock market." His fuller line is "know what you own" — [Yahoo Finance 2023](https://ca.finance.yahoo.com/news/fidelity-legend-peter-lynch-i-never-said-to-invest-in-the-stock-market-120755736.html); [CapexCat](https://capexcat.substack.com/p/peter-lynch-buy-what-you-know)
- After Lynch, Magellan returned 7.8%/yr from 1990 to mid-2010, 0.4%/yr behind the S&P. Scale ($14B growing to $50B+) eroded the edge — [CBS MoneyWatch](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/lessons-from-a-great-fund-managers-record)

### Inferences
- **Replicable:** the habit of turning over many rocks (visits, reports, field observation), staying humble about market timing, holding a winner through doubt (Chrysler), and keeping enough diversification that a few tenbaggers drive results.
- **Not replicable or circumstantial:** a professional analyst team plus open access to management, before Regulation FD (2000) limited selective disclosure. A tiny starting fund ($18–22M) was closed to inflows until 1981, the strongest years coincided with the start of the 1982–87 bull market, and the workload burned him out by 46.
- "Buy what you know" in the popular sense, spotting a product at the mall, was only an idea source. The actual work was visits plus balance-sheet reading at about 200+ companies a year.
- The investor-returns story teaches more about investor behavior (chasing after good years) than about Lynch. The "lost money" version should not be repeated as fact.

### Gaps
- The Chrysler "5% by July 1982" and Fannie Mae "$500M" figures each rest on a single secondary source (CapexCat). I could not verify them in *Beating the Street* this session.
- I found no primary source for "80+ hour weeks." The documented figures (about 6:45 a.m. arrival, about 13-hour days, Saturdays, a week a month traveling) imply roughly 70–80 hours.
- I found no documented methodology for the CBS 13.4% figure or for the CapexCat 17.4% estimate.

## The real replicable lesson vs. myth

### Takeaway
Both men won through enormous volumes of primary research applied to small, under-followed opportunities while their capital was small. Scale shrank both of their edges. The most-repeated anecdotes (cigar-butt brilliance, "buy what you know," "Magellan investors lost money") get their causes wrong. Buffett's lifetime results come largely from cheap, durable leverage and decades of survival. Lynch's come from relentless legwork during a favorable 13-year window.

### Cited Findings
- Buffett: "a huge structural advantage not to have a lot of money"; 50%/yr possible on $1M (1999) — [AOL/Moneywise](https://www.aol.com/finance/warren-buffett-certain-could-earn-121300309.html)
- Berkshire's alpha disappears after controlling for BAB and QMJ factors plus 1.7x leverage. The Sharpe ratio is 0.79, "very good but not superhuman" — [Buffett's Alpha](https://gwern.net/doc/economics/2018-frazzini.pdf)
- Lynch's post-1990 Magellan, at large scale, lagged the S&P — [CBS MoneyWatch](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/lessons-from-a-great-fund-managers-record)
- Lynch: "the average person could know three or four or five companies very well" — [PBS Frontline](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/pages/frontline/shows/betting/pros/lynch.html)

### Inferences
- **Myth vs. fact table** for the writer:
  - "Buffett got rich from cigar butts" — false in dollars. Cigar butts built his seed capital. The wealth came from leveraged, float-funded quality compounding, mostly after age 50–65.
  - "Buffett's alpha is magic or luck" — neither, per AQR. It is factor exposure, cheap leverage and survival, and he spotted those factors decades before academics did.
  - "Buffett bet 40% on Amex" — plausible and widely reported, but not verified here against the primary letter.
  - "Lynch: buy what you know" — oversimplified. He meant "know what you own," backed by 200+ visits a year.
  - "Most Magellan investors lost money" — no study exists. Fidelity denied it. What happened is a dollar-weighted shortfall.
- **The most transferable lesson for a small investor:** a small capital base is a real edge in obscure securities if paired with Buffett- or Lynch-level reading and visit volume, plus a structure that never forces selling, meaning no margin calls and no redemptions.

### Gaps
- I found no rigorous study separating luck from skill for Lynch comparable to *Buffett's Alpha* (factor-adjusted alpha). The 7.9% alpha figure is from a secondary calculation.
