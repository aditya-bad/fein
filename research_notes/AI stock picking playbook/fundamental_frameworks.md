# Fundamental Stock-Picking Frameworks of Legendary Investors and the Valuation Metrics They Use

Research notes, compiled 2026-11-19. They are written for a retail investor in India who also buys US stocks.

**How to read these notes.** "Said" means the investor's own words or primary documents, such as shareholder letters, books and fund materials. "Commentary" means summaries by third parties, such as blogs, brokers and podcasts. Some sources were opened or surfaced in this session through search; for those, the search snippets were read. Other citations are well-known primary documents (Berkshire letters, classic books) cited from prior knowledge and **not re-fetched this session**. Each of these is marked **[primary, not re-fetched]**. Check the exact wording before quoting any of them in a published report.

## 1. Western investors: stated selection criteria

### Takeaway
Most of the legends share a common core. They want a business they understand, one that earns high and durable returns on capital (a "moat"), run by honest owner-minded management, and bought at a price below a conservative estimate of intrinsic value. They then hold it for a long time. The investors differ mainly in where they look:
- Graham looks at cheap assets and earnings.
- Fisher, Lynch and Mayer look at growth.
- Sleep looks at scale economics that the company shares with customers.
- Pabrai looks at asymmetric bets.
- Smith looks at high ROCE combined with "do nothing".

### Cited Findings

**Warren Buffett (Berkshire Hathaway letters)**
- **Four filters (said).** Berkshire wants a business "(1) that we can understand; (2) with favorable long-term prospects; (3) operated by honest and competent people; and (4) available at a very attractive price." — [1977 Berkshire letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/1977.html) [primary, not re-fetched]
- **Acquisition criteria (said).** These are printed in each annual report:
  - "demonstrated consistent earning power (future projections are of no interest to us, nor are 'turnaround' situations)"
  - "businesses earning good returns on equity while employing little or no debt"
  - "management in place"
  - "simple businesses (if there's lots of technology, we won't understand it)"
  - "an offering price"
  - Source: [Berkshire annual letters, e.g. 1997](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/1997.html) [primary, not re-fetched]
- **Owner earnings (said).** Buffett defines owner earnings as "(a) reported earnings plus (b) depreciation, depletion, amortization, and certain other non-cash charges ... less (c) the average annual amount of capitalized expenditures for plant and equipment, etc. that the business requires to fully maintain its long-term competitive position and its unit volume." The formula is therefore Net income + D&A + other non-cash charges − maintenance capex (± any working capital the business needs to hold its position). — [1986 Berkshire letter (appendix on Scott Fetzer purchase accounting)](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/1986.html) [primary, not re-fetched]
- **Wonderful company at a fair price (said).** "It's far better to buy a wonderful company at a fair price than a fair company at a wonderful price." — [1989 Berkshire letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/1989.html) [primary, not re-fetched]
- **Circle of competence (said).** Buffett's ideal is to buy "at a rational price, a part interest in an easily-understandable business whose earnings are virtually certain to be materially higher five, ten and twenty years from now." On the circle of competence: "The size of that circle is not very important; knowing its boundaries, however, is vital." — [1996 Berkshire letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/1996.html) [primary, not re-fetched]
- **Moats (said).** "A truly great business must have an enduring 'moat' that protects excellent returns on invested capital." In the same letter Buffett sorts businesses into "great, good, gruesome". See's Candies is the great example: it needs little capital to grow. — [2007 Berkshire letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/2007ltr.pdf) [primary, not re-fetched]
- **Intrinsic value and growth (said).** Intrinsic value is the discounted value of the cash that can be taken out of a business over its remaining life. Buffett says growth and value are "joined at the hip": growth only adds value when the return on incremental capital is above its cost. — [1992 Berkshire letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/1992.html) [primary, not re-fetched]

**Charlie Munger**
- **Mental models (said).** Munger argues for a "latticework of mental models" drawn from several disciplines. He also says: "Over the long term, it's hard for a stock to earn a much better return than the business which underlies it earns. If the business earns 6% on capital over 40 years and you hold it for that 40 years, you're not going to make much different than a 6% return—even if you originally buy it at a huge discount. Conversely, if a business earns 18% on capital over 20 or 30 years, even if you pay an expensive looking price, you'll end up with one hell of a result." — "A Lesson on Elementary Worldly Wisdom," USC Business School, 1994 (transcript widely reproduced, e.g. [Farnam Street](https://fs.blog/great-talks/a-lesson-on-worldly-wisdom/)) [primary, not re-fetched]
- **Inversion (said).** "Invert, always invert" (borrowed from the mathematician Jacobi). In practice you ask what would make the investment fail and then avoid those conditions. The bias catalogue is in "The Psychology of Human Misjudgment" (1995). [primary, not re-fetched]
- **Munger's influence on Smith (commentary).** Fundsmith's approach has been described as "the Charlie Munger approach." — [GuruFocus](https://www.gurufocus.com/news/768842/fundsmith-the-charlie-munger-approach)

**Buffett's results as factor exposure (academic, see section 4)**
- Berkshire's Sharpe ratio was 0.79. Its alpha "becomes insignificant when controlling for exposures to Betting-Against-Beta and Quality-Minus-Junk factors." The authors conclude that Buffett's returns are a "reward for leveraging cheap, safe, quality stocks," with average leverage of about 1.7:1. — [Frazzini, Kabiller & Pedersen, "Buffett's Alpha," NBER w19681](https://www.nber.org/papers/w19681.pdf); [FAJ 2018](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/en/research/financial-analysts-journal/2018/faj-v74-n4-3)

**Benjamin Graham**
- **Margin of safety (said).** "Margin of Safety" is the central concept of investment, set out in chapter 20 of *The Intelligent Investor*. [primary, not re-fetched]
- **Defensive investor criteria (said, ch. 14 of *The Intelligent Investor*, revised ed.).** [primary, not re-fetched]
  - adequate size
  - strong financial condition: current ratio ≥ 2, and long-term debt no greater than net current assets
  - earnings stability: positive earnings in each of the past 10 years
  - uninterrupted dividends for 20 years
  - EPS growth of at least one-third over 10 years, using 3-year averages at each end
  - P/E ≤ 15 on 3-year average earnings
  - P/B ≤ 1.5, or P/E × P/B ≤ 22.5
- **The Graham Number.** It is √(22.5 × EPS × BVPS), derived from the last criterion above.

**Philip Fisher (*Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits*, 1958)** [primary, not re-fetched]
- **The 15 points (said, paraphrased).**
  1. Products or services with enough market potential for a sizable sales increase over several years
  2. Management determined to develop new products once current ones are exhausted
  3. Effective R&D relative to size
  4. An above-average sales organisation
  5. A worthwhile profit margin
  6. Work to maintain or improve margins
  7. Outstanding labour and personnel relations
  8. Outstanding executive relations
  9. Depth to management
  10. Good cost analysis and accounting controls
  11. Industry-specific competitive advantages
  12. A long-range outlook on profits
  13. Growth that will not need equity financing large enough to dilute existing holders much
  14. Management that talks freely to investors when things go wrong
  15. Management of unquestionable integrity
- **Scuttlebutt (said).** Gather information about a company from customers, suppliers, competitors, ex-employees and researchers rather than only from reports.
- **Selling (said).** Fisher's view: "If the job has been correctly done when a common stock is purchased, the time to sell it is—almost never."

**Peter Lynch (*One Up on Wall Street*, 1989)**
- **Six categories (said).** Slow growers, stalwarts, fast growers, cyclicals, turnarounds and asset plays. — [Value Research](https://www.valueresearchonline.com/stories/51021/peter-lynch-s-six-stock-categories/); [Dividend Power](https://www.dividendpower.org/peter-lynchs-six-stock-categories/)
  - Slow growers have earnings growth about equal to GDP and high, steady dividends (commentary).
  - In Lynch's book, stalwarts grow about 10–12% a year and fast growers about 20–25% a year [primary, not re-fetched].
- **PEG (said).** "The P/E ratio of any company that's fairly priced will equal its growth rate." Half the growth rate is "very positive"; twice the growth rate is "very negative" [primary, not re-fetched].
  - Lynch's dividend-adjusted version is (EPS growth + dividend yield) / P/E. On this measure, below 1 is poor, 1.5 is okay and 2 or more is attractive [primary, not re-fetched].
  - The commonly used cutoffs are PEG 1.0 = fair and <1 = cheap. Practitioners put "expensive" at >1.5 or >2. — [Value Research/secondary summaries](https://www.valueresearchonline.com/stories/51021/peter-lynch-s-six-stock-categories/)
  - Caution: "a PEG below 1 is not proof that the stock is undervalued." — [Validea blog](https://blog.validea.com/?p=32501)
- **Other ideas (said).** "Invest in what you know": use your edge as a consumer or professional, then do the research. "Tenbagger" is Lynch's coined term for a 10x stock.

**Mohnish Pabrai (*The Dhandho Investor*, 2007)**
- **The nine principles.** They include investing in existing businesses, simple businesses, distressed businesses in distressed industries, businesses with durable moats, few bets/big bets/infrequent bets, arbitrage, margin of safety, and "low risk, high uncertainty." — [Manas Saloi book summary](https://manassaloi.com/booksummaries/2016/03/14/mohnish-pabrai-the-dhandho-investor.html); [GuruFocus](https://www.gurufocus.com/news/338613/the-dhandho-framework)
- **Asymmetry (said).** "Heads I win; tails I don't lose much." — [Outlook Money](https://www.outlookmoney.com/invest/mohnish-pabrais-lessons-in-the-dhandho-of-investing)
- **Cloning (said).** Pabrai calls himself a "shameless cloner" of Buffett, Munger and others. — [Antoine Buteau, lessons from Pabrai](https://www.antoinebuteau.com/lessons-from-mohnish-pabrai/)
- **Evolution (commentary).** Later commentary says his approach has shifted toward growth with a value bent. — [Outlook Money](https://www.outlookmoney.com/invest/mohnish-pabrais-lessons-in-the-dhandho-of-investing)

**Terry Smith (Fundsmith)**
- **Mantra (said).** "Buy good companies, don't overpay, do nothing." Fundsmith: "We only buy shares in good companies, try not to overpay and then do nothing." — [Fundsmith](https://www.fundsmith.co.uk/news/2015/2171-this-is-money-firms-which-provide-good-products-or-services-are-key-to-investing-says-fundsmith-boss/); [Smithson/Fidelity "three steps"](https://smithson.co.uk/news/2015/2036-fidelity-three-steps-to-heaven)
- **Primary test.** The key test is a high ROCE across the economic cycle. Holdings typically earn 25–30% ROCE. — [Trustnet](https://www.trustnet.com/news/662915/three-simple-steps-to-making-returns-like-terry-smith)
- **Conflicting thresholds.** Blogs cite 15–20% or "20%+" ROCE, and one lists interest coverage >15x. These are lower-quality secondary sources. — [MarketScreener](https://in.marketscreener.com/news/latest/Legendary-investor-Terry-Smith-44733685)
- Fundsmith's annual owner letters also stress high gross margins, high cash conversion and a FCF yield-based valuation [primary, not re-fetched].

**Nick Sleep and Qais Zakaria (Nomad Investment Partnership, 2001–2014)**
- **Core idea.** The idea began as "scale efficiencies shared" and was later renamed "scale economics shared." The firm passes the savings from scale to customers through lower prices. This drives volume, which brings more scale and still lower costs. Costco and Amazon are the canonical examples. — [Morningstar AU](https://www.morningstar.com.au/insights/personal-finance/254055/my-favourite-lessons-from-nick-sleeps-famous-investing-letters); [Chit Chat Stocks](https://chitchatstocks.substack.com/p/nick-sleep-how-the-secretive-investor)
- **Returns.** One source gives about 921% cumulative versus 116.9% for MSCI World (2001–2013). Another gives about 18.4%/yr. — [Chit Chat Stocks](https://chitchatstocks.substack.com/p/nick-sleep-how-the-secretive-investor). These figures differ by source; verify against the letters (the IGY Foundation PDF).
- **Other lessons.** Concentrate, distrust "massive over-diversification", and rely on patient capital. — [Rabi Chatterjee summary](https://rabichatterjee.substack.com/p/learnings-from-nomad-investment-partnership)

**Thomas Phelps (*100 to 1 in the Stock Market*, 1972) and Chris Mayer (*100 Baggers*, 2015)**
- **Phelps.** He studied 365 stocks that returned 100:1 between 1932 and 1971, and his advice was to "buy right and hold on." Commentary notes his work was more anecdotal than statistical. — [Hedge Fund Alpha, Mayer notes](https://hedgefundalpha.com/strategies/christopher-mayers-notes-100-baggers/); [MicroCap Club review](https://microcapclub.com/book-review-100-baggers-by-chris-mayer/)
- **Mayer's study.** He studied US 100-baggers from 1962 to 2014. — [Hedge Fund Alpha](https://hedgefundalpha.com/strategies/christopher-mayers-notes-100-baggers/)
- **Mayer's key idea, the "twin engines".** EPS growth combined with a rising P/E multiple. — [Validea](https://blog.validea.com/?p=33794)
- **Mayer's other traits.**
  - high ROE sustained over long periods
  - owner-operators and "intelligent fanatics"
  - a smaller starting size
  - a moat
  - a reasonable starting multiple
  - a "coffee can" holding discipline, after Robert Kirby: buy, put away and leave alone for about 10 years. Mayer calls it "a triumph of lethargy and sloth."
  - Sources: [Mindful Compounding](https://mindfulcompounding.substack.com/p/what-i-learned-from-chris-mayer); [Jimmy's Journal checklist](https://jimmysjournal.substack.com/p/how-to-spot-the-next-100-bagger-before)

### Inferences
- A combined Western checklist would ask:
  - Is it understandable (inside your circle of competence)?
  - Is ROCE/ROIC above 15–20% over a full cycle?
  - Is reinvestment runway long (Fisher #1, Mayer, Sleep)?
  - Is debt low?
  - Are management honest owner-operators?
  - Is the price at or below a conservative intrinsic value, judged by an owner-earnings or FCF yield?
  - Will you hold for years?
- Graham's rules work as a safety screen, for balance sheet and earnings stability. They are less useful for finding compounders: modern high-ROIC, asset-light firms rarely trade below 1.5x book.
- Munger's 6% vs 18% quote is the conceptual bridge from Graham (cheap) to Buffett/Smith (quality). Over long holding periods, the return on capital dominates the entry multiple.

### Gaps
- Fisher, Lynch and Graham details come from the books as cited from memory, and the exact quotes were not re-fetched. Check the wording against the original editions before you publish.
- The Nomad letters PDF was not opened, so the exact return figures are unverified and differ by source.

## 2. Indian investors: stated rules

### Takeaway
India's best-documented frameworks are Motilal Oswal/Raamdeo Agrawal's **QGLP/SQGLP**, backed by 30 years of Wealth Creation Studies; Marcellus's **10-year twin filter** (revenue growth >10% and ROCE >15% every year, plus forensic accounting checks); and Vijay Kedia's **SMILE**. The other well-known individuals (Jhunjhunwala, Kacholia, Khanna, Veliyath, Maheshwari) have **no formal published checklist**. Their "rules" are inferred from interviews and from portfolio disclosures.

### Cited Findings

**Raamdeo Agrawal / Motilal Oswal: QGLP and the Wealth Creation Studies**
- **QGLP (said by the firm).** Quality of business and management, Growth in earnings, Longevity of quality and growth, and a favourable Price. "Buy Right" means "buying quality companies at a reasonable price," and "Sit Tight" means staying invested to realise full growth potential. The firm frames the price leg as buying a good business at a fair price, not a fair business at a good price. — [Motilal Oswal PMS investment philosophy](https://pms.motilaloswalmf.com/about-us/investment-philosophy)
- **100x study (2014).** [primary, not re-fetched]
  - Results in the study: about 47 stocks did 100x over the study span, out of a universe of about 3,500. On average they took about 12 years, which is roughly a 47% CAGR. Most 100x stocks gave multi-year windows to buy in. — [Outlook Business interview](https://www.outlookbusiness.com/interview/how-to-spot-a-100-bagger-744); [Hedge Fund Alpha, "100x power of growth"](https://hedgefundalpha.com/strategies/100x-power-growth-wealth-creation/)
  - Framework: the study added **S (Size)** to make **SQGLP**, favouring a small market cap at the start. Agrawal's "100x" formula is the twin engine again: earnings growth combined with P/E rerating.
- **Agrawal's own framing (said).** He says "100X is a rare phenomenon." — [Outlook Business](https://www.outlookbusiness.com/markets/interview/value-investors-do-not-talk-or-focus-too-much-on-growth-2482)
- **QGLP screen pass rate.** One QGLP screen kept only 24 of 531 investible companies (<5%). — [5paisa summary of Agrawal's philosophy](https://www.5paisa.com/blog/raamdeo-agrawal-investment-philosophy-and-latest-portfolio-2026)
- **Long-run lessons (said by the firm).** Across the studies, Motilal repeatedly states three lessons:
  - Wealth is created when you buy at low P/E and the stock then rerates: "the biggest wealth is created when growth and rerating combine."
  - "Value migration" across sectors.
  - High returns on equity combined with earnings growth.
  - Source: [Wealth Creation Study 2009–14](https://www.motilaloswal.com/site/rreports/HTML/635542588369519988/index.htm) [primary, opened only as a search result]
- **30th study (Dec 2025, covering 2020–25).**
  - The top 100 wealth creators added a record ₹148 trillion.
  - Wealth creation grew at a 38% CAGR, against 21% for the Sensex.
  - The top 10 fastest creators returned about 88% CAGR, against 24% for the Nifty TRI: ₹1 crore became ₹24 crore.
  - Financials were the largest sector, followed by industrials, capital markets, technology and utilities.
  - The theme is "India – The Multi-Trillion Dollar Opportunity." The study expects large caps to outperform in the medium term.
  - Sources: [Motilal Oswal press release](https://www.motilaloswalgroup.com/Media-Room/Press-Release/-/india’s-biggest-wealth-boom-rs148-trillion-ad/FS/20796); [Thematic study Dec 2025](https://www.motilaloswal.com/site/rreports/html/639014785692549159/index.htm); [Outlook Money](https://www.outlookmoney.com/amp/story/invest/biggest-and-fastest-wealth-creators-since-2020-top-stocks-that-turned-rs-1-crore-into-rs-24-crore)
  - These are Motilal's own calculations and were not independently verified.

**Saurabh Mukherjea / Marcellus: Consistent Compounders (CCP)**
- **Twin filter (said).** Double-digit year-on-year revenue growth **and** a return on capital above the cost of capital, **every year for 10 consecutive years**. The filter is applied annually. — [Marcellus CCP help doc](https://marcellus.helpscoutdocs.com/article/30-marcellus-consistent-compounders-portfolio-ccp); [Marcellus FAQ](https://marcellus.in/pms-faqs/what-is-the-difference-between-marcellus-consistent-compounders-portfolio-and-the-filter-based-robotic-portfolio-explained-in-the-books-coffee-can-investing-and-unusual-billionaires/)
  - In *Coffee Can Investing* and the Nifty50 analysis, this is made concrete as revenue growth >10% and ROCE >15% in every year of FY08–18. — [Marcellus newsletter](https://marcellus.in/newsletter/consistent-compounders/valuing-longevity-of-healthy-fundamentals/)
- **From screen to portfolio.** The screen produces about 25–35 names. Bottom-up moat research then narrows these to 12–15 stocks. "Excusable blips" are forgiven; an example is Nestlé India's Maggi-hit FY15. — [Marcellus FAQ](https://marcellus.in/faqs)
- **Claimed outperformance.** Marcellus says it adds about 4–5% a year over the pure-filter portfolio. This is a firm claim and was not verified. — [Marcellus FAQ](https://marcellus.in/pms-faqs/what-is-the-difference-between-marcellus-consistent-compounders-portfolio-and-the-filter-based-robotic-portfolio-explained-in-the-books-coffee-can-investing-and-unusual-billionaires/)
- **Forensic accounting screens.** Marcellus also runs forensic accounting screens. Reported items include:
  - CFO/EBITDA
  - contingent liabilities / net worth
  - auditor remuneration growth
  - related-party transactions
  - capital work-in-progress / gross block
  - yield on cash
  - depreciation-rate volatility
  - [Marcellus newsletters, "forensic accounting"](https://marcellus.in/newsletter/consistent-compounders/risk-reward-arbitrage-of-investing-in-quality/) [details from prior knowledge; exact ratio list not re-fetched]

**Vijay Kedia: SMILE**
- **SMILE (said).** "Small in size, Medium in experience, Large in aspiration, Extra-large in market potential." — [BloombergQuint/NDTV Profit](https://www.bloombergquint.com/markets/this-dalal-street-veterans-secret-to-investing-is-smile); [Value Research](https://www.valueresearchonline.com/stories/54019/how-to-invest-like-vijay-kedia/)
  - Equentis renders the "L" as "large desire."
  - Commentary notes that SMILE is not a valuation model and that management quality is the foundation. — [sharpely](https://sharpely.in/blogs/vijay-kedia-investing-philosophy/)
- **Valuation (Kedia).** Value Research reports that he stresses balancing valuation and return, because good companies are rarely cheap. — [Value Research](https://www.valueresearchonline.com/stories/54019/how-to-invest-like-vijay-kedia/)

**Ashish Kacholia**
- He focuses on small and microcaps and takes a "buying a business" approach. He assesses management's integrity, potential and capability, looks for moats and generally buys and holds. He rarely gives interviews. — [Noiseless Trader](https://thenoiselesstrader.beehiiv.com/p/beyond-the-headlines-the-low-profile-strategy-of-ashish-kacholia); [Kotak Securities](https://www.kotaksecurities.com/investing-guide/articles/cloning-the-superstar-of-india) (commentary)

**Basant Maheshwari (*The Thoughtful Investor*)**
- He looks for predictable earnings streams in sectors with strong tailwinds. The book covers both creating and retaining wealth. He has said "No fixed rules here." — [PMS AIF World](https://www.pmsaifworld.com/?p=6444); [Moneyexcel](https://moneyexcel.com/basant-maheshwari-investment-strategy-advice/) (commentary)
- He is widely associated with growth investing and with avoiding PSUs and commodity businesses [unverified].

**Porinju Veliyath (Equity Intelligence)**
- His focus is undervalued small and mid caps. He says "all sensible investing is value investing." He is described as a high-risk contrarian. — [inkl/Equity Intelligence profile](https://www.inkl.com/news/meet-the-risky-investor-equity-intelligence-s-porinju-veliyath); [Groww](https://groww.in/blog/successful-investors-indian-stock-market/) (commentary)

**Dolly Khanna**
- Her husband Rajiv Khanna manages the portfolio. It holds lesser-known, under-the-radar small caps, often in unglamorous manufacturing, chemicals and sugar, with strengthening fundamentals, and is classified as growth style. — [Pocketful](https://www.pocketful.in/blog/top-investors-in-india-and-their-portfolios); [Alice Blue](https://aliceblueonline.com/blog/hindi/porinju-v-veliyath-portfolio-vs-dolly-khanna-portfolio-hindi) (commentary)

**Rakesh Jhunjhunwala (d. 2022)**
- No primary source for a formal checklist was found in this session. Widely attributed themes [unverified] include:
  - a long-term "India growth story" conviction
  - a mix of trading and investing
  - big concentrated bets such as Titan, CRISIL and Lupin
  - management quality first
  - "buy right, sit tight" (more correctly Motilal's phrase)

### Inferences
- A combined Indian checklist would include:
  - 10 years of revenue growth ≥10% and ROCE ≥15% every year (Marcellus).
  - CFO/EBITDA ≥ about 70–80% over 5–10 years, with clean forensic flags.
  - Promoter integrity: low pledge, sensible related-party transactions, a credible auditor.
  - A small size with a large runway (SQGLP/SMILE).
  - An entry P/E that leaves room for rerating (QGLP "P").
  - A long holding period.
- Motilal's evidence across its studies and Mayer's US 100-bagger work point to the same mechanism. The biggest winners came from earnings compounding *plus* multiple expansion. Paying a very high starting P/E removes the second engine.
- The 30th study's sector mix (financials, industrials, capital markets) reflects the 2020–25 cycle. It should not be read as a permanent rule.

### Gaps
- No original QGLP or 100x PDF was opened this session. The exact SQGLP thresholds were not verified: Motilal has used figures such as ROE >20%, EPS growth >20% and PEG <1, but these are unconfirmed.
- No reliable primary sources were found for formal rules from Jhunjhunwala, Khanna or Veliyath. Their "rules" exist mostly as media interpretations of portfolio disclosures.
- The precise Marcellus forensic ratio list and weights were not re-verified.

## 3. Valuation and quality metrics: formulas and practitioner thresholds

### Takeaway
Use quality metrics (ROCE/ROIC, cash conversion, leverage) to decide *whether* to own a business. Use valuation metrics (FCF yield, EV/EBITDA, PEG, reverse DCF) to decide *at what price*. The most robust valuation discipline for a retail investor is the **reverse DCF**: ask what growth the price implies, then judge whether that is plausible.

### Cited Findings
- **Reverse DCF / expectations investing (Mauboussin & Rappaport).**
  - The method starts from the current price and solves for the expectations it implies (price-implied expectations, or PIE). It does not forecast and discount. — [Motley Fool Q&A with Mauboussin & Rappaport](https://www.fool.com/investing/2022/01/19/expectations-investing-qanda-mauboussin-rappaport); [Mauboussin book excerpt PDF](https://www.michaelmauboussin.com/s/EIexcerpt.pdf)
  - The value drivers are revenue growth, operating (NOPBT) margin and investment rate. The value determinants are cash tax rate, WACC and the market-implied forecast period (the market-implied competitive advantage period, MICAP). — [CFA Path summary](https://cfapath.substack.com/p/expectations-investing); [Cowboy Computer review](https://cowboycomputer.substack.com/p/book-review-expectations-investing)
  - The process has three steps: (1) estimate PIE; (2) identify the "expectations opportunities" by finding the value trigger (sales, costs or investment) with the biggest impact; (3) buy or sell when your view differs from PIE.
  - A free reverse DCF spreadsheet is on the companion site. A DCF can understate the value of optionality, so the authors suggest adding real-options analysis for highly uncertain firms. — [Motley Fool AU](https://www.fool.com.au/2022/01/20/expectations-investing-a-qampa-with-michael-mauboussin-and-alfred-rappaport-usfeed/)
- **Twin or double engine.** Total return ≈ (1+EPS growth) × (1+change in P/E) × … + dividend yield. 100-baggers need both EPS growth and multiple expansion. — [Validea on Mayer](https://blog.validea.com/?p=33794); [Hedge Fund Alpha on Motilal 100x](https://hedgefundalpha.com/strategies/100x-power-growth-wealth-creation/)
- **PEG.** PEG = P/E ÷ EPS growth rate (%). Lynch treats 1 as fair value; practitioners treat <1 as cheap and >1.5–2 as expensive. — [Value Research](https://www.valueresearchonline.com/stories/51021/peter-lynch-s-six-stock-categories/)
- **ROCE at Fundsmith.** Holdings typically earn 25–30%. — [Trustnet](https://www.trustnet.com/news/662915/three-simple-steps-to-making-returns-like-terry-smith)
- **Marcellus thresholds.** ROCE >15% and revenue growth >10% in every year for 10 years. — [Marcellus newsletter](https://marcellus.in/newsletter/consistent-compounders/valuing-longevity-of-healthy-fundamentals/)
- **Owner earnings formula.** See section 1. — [1986 Berkshire letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/1986.html)
- **Graham thresholds.** P/E ≤15, P/B ≤1.5, P/E×P/B ≤22.5, current ratio ≥2. See section 1. [primary, not re-fetched]

**Standard formula reference.** These are textbook definitions, for example from Damodaran's [Investment Valuation resources at NYU Stern](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/). The thresholds are common practitioner conventions and are not tied to one source unless noted. [not re-fetched]

| Metric | Formula | Common practitioner thresholds / notes |
|---|---|---|
| Trailing P/E | Price ÷ last-12-month EPS | Backward-looking; distorted by one-offs. |
| Forward P/E | Price ÷ next-12-month or next-FY consensus EPS | Depends on analyst estimates, which tend to be optimistic. Compare it with trailing P/E: a much lower forward P/E implies the market expects growth or a recovery. |
| PEG | P/E ÷ EPS growth % | <1 cheap; 1 fair; >2 expensive (Lynch). Use 3–5 yr growth, not 1 yr. |
| EV/EBITDA | (Mkt cap + debt + minorities + prefs − cash) ÷ EBITDA | Capital-structure neutral; good for comparing peers. About 8–12x is "average" for mature non-financials, but this varies widely by sector. Not meaningful for banks/NBFCs. |
| ROE | Net income ÷ avg shareholders' equity | Buffett-style quality: >15–20% sustained with low debt. Leverage inflates it, so check it alongside D/E (DuPont: margin × asset turnover × leverage). |
| ROCE | EBIT ÷ (total assets − current liabilities) = EBIT ÷ (equity + debt) | >15% (Marcellus floor); 20%+ high quality; 25–30% Fundsmith-type. Should exceed the cost of capital: about 12–14% in India, about 8–10% in the US. |
| ROIC | NOPAT ÷ invested capital, where NOPAT = EBIT×(1−t) and invested capital = net working capital + net fixed assets (+ goodwill) | The value creation spread is ROIC − WACC. Incremental ROIC matters most: Δ NOPAT ÷ Δ invested capital. |
| FCF | CFO − capex | |
| FCF yield | FCF ÷ market cap (or unlevered FCF ÷ EV) | >5% attractive for mature firms; compare with the 10-yr G-sec (about 6.5–7% in India) or the US Treasury yield. |
| Owner earnings | NI + D&A + non-cash charges − maintenance capex (± required working capital) | Buffett 1986. Maintenance capex is unreported; a proxy is roughly D&A, or Greenwald's method (capex − growth capex estimated from the PP&E/sales ratio × Δsales). |
| DCF | Σ FCFₜ/(1+WACC)ᵗ + TV/(1+WACC)ⁿ, where TV = FCFₙ₊₁/(WACC − g) | Terminal value often accounts for 60–80% of the total, so it is very sensitive to g and WACC. Keep g ≤ long-run nominal GDP. |
| Reverse DCF | Solve for the growth rate or advantage period that sets DCF = current price | Mauboussin. Ask: is the implied growth plausible against base rates? |
| Operating leverage (DOL) | %Δ EBIT ÷ %Δ revenue | High DOL means earnings swing sharply as sales grow or shrink, which matters for cyclicals and for turnarounds with high fixed costs. |
| Cash conversion | CFO ÷ EBITDA (or CFO ÷ PAT) | CFO/EBITDA ≥70–80% over 5+ yrs is healthy; persistently <50% is a forensic red flag (Marcellus-style). FCF/PAT near 100% suits asset-light firms. |
| Working capital cycle (CCC) | Debtor days + inventory days − payable days, where debtor days = receivables÷sales×365, inventory days = inventory÷COGS×365 and payable days = payables÷COGS×365 | Lower or negative is better. Rising debtor days while revenue grows is a red flag. |
| Debt/Equity | Total debt ÷ shareholders' equity | <0.5 conservative; <1 acceptable for non-financials. Buffett's language is "little or no debt." Use net debt/EBITDA <2x as a cross-check. |
| Interest coverage | EBIT ÷ interest expense | >5x comfortable; >10–15x quality (one source puts Smith at >15x, unverified); <2–3x danger. |

### Inferences
- **For Indian stocks.** The cost of capital is higher (about 12–14% nominal in INR), so a "15% ROCE" bar is the *minimum* needed to create value, not a sign of excellence. US firms need a lower bar of about 10%+ ROIC.
- **Forward vs trailing P/E.** Forward P/E builds in analyst optimism. A practical rule is to compute both and then run a reverse DCF. If the price only works with growth above the company's 10-year history, the margin of safety is thin.
- **Banks and NBFCs.** EV/EBITDA, ROCE and the cash-conversion metrics do not apply to financials. Use P/B against ROE (justified P/B ≈ (ROE − g)/(Ke − g)), together with asset quality (GNPA/NNPA), credit cost and cost of funds. This is relevant because financials led the 30th Motilal study.

### Gaps
- No single authoritative source gives "standard" thresholds for EV/EBITDA, FCF yield or interest coverage. The numbers above are common conventions, not research-backed cutoffs.
- Mauboussin's primary PDFs were not fully read in this session; only the excerpt and reviews were surfaced.

## 4. Academic evidence on value, quality and profitability factors

### Takeaway
The academic evidence broadly supports the legends' ideas:
- Cheap stocks (value) and profitable, high-quality stocks have historically earned premiums.
- Profitability and value are negatively correlated, so combining them works better than either alone. This is "quality at a reasonable price," which is what Buffett/Munger and QGLP practise.
- Buffett's own record is largely explained by leveraged exposure to cheap, safe, quality stocks.

### Cited Findings
- **Fama-French factors.** The 3-factor model (1993) adds size (SMB) and value (HML) to the market factor. The 5-factor model (2015) adds profitability (RMW, robust minus weak operating profitability) and investment (CMA, conservative minus aggressive asset growth). In the 5-factor model, HML becomes largely redundant once RMW and CMA are included. — [Fama & French 2015, JFE, SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2287202) [primary, not re-fetched]
- **Novy-Marx, "The Other Side of Value" (JFE 2013).**
  - Gross profits ÷ assets predicts returns about as well as book-to-market.
  - Profitability strategies are strongly negatively correlated with value, so combining the two is powerful. The sample covers 1962–2010.
  - The premium is not simply explained by risk: profitable firms are less prone to distress.
  - Sources: [Novy-Marx paper (Ivey copy)](https://www.ivey.uwo.ca/media/3775548/novy-marx.pdf); [Cochrane-hosted copy](https://www.johnhcochrane.com/s/Novy_marx_OSoV.pdf); [Swedroe/ETF.com](https://www.etf.com/node/94104.md)
- **Asness, Frazzini & Pedersen, "Quality Minus Junk."**
  - Quality is defined from profitability, growth, safety and payout.
  - QMJ (long quality, short junk) earns significant risk-adjusted returns in the US and across 24 countries.
  - The price of quality varies over time: quality stocks are not always priced high enough relative to their fundamentals.
  - Sources: [NBER conference paper](https://conference.nber.org/conf_papers/f69835.pdf); [Asness slides, Wharton Jacobs Levy](https://jacobslevycenter.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2015/05/5-Cliff-Asness1.pdf)
- **"Size Matters, if You Control Your Junk" (Asness et al., JFE 2018).** The size premium becomes robust once quality is controlled for, because small junk stocks drag it down. — [CBS publisher version](https://research-api.cbs.dk/ws/files/57307999/lasse_heje_pedersen_et_al_size_matters_if_you_control_your_junk_publishersversion.pdf)
- **"Buffett's Alpha" (Frazzini, Kabiller & Pedersen; NBER 2013, FAJ 2018).**
  - Sharpe ratio 0.79; leverage about 1.7:1 on average (about 1.6:1 in the 2013 draft).
  - Alpha becomes insignificant after controlling for BAB (betting-against-beta, i.e. low risk) and QMJ.
  - The authors' conclusion is "neither luck nor magic," but a reward for leveraging cheap, safe, quality stocks.
  - Sources: [NBER w19681](https://www.nber.org/papers/w19681.pdf); [SSRN 3197185](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=3197185); [CBS](https://research.cbs.dk/en/publications/buffetts-alpha/)

### Inferences
- **For a retail checklist.** A combination of profitability (ROCE/ROIC, gross profit/assets), safety (low leverage, low volatility, stable earnings) and value (FCF yield, EV/EBITDA) is the academically defensible core. Of these, profitability and safety are most aligned with what Buffett did.
- **Small caps.** "Size matters if you control your junk" supports the small-cap tilt in Indian frameworks (SQGLP's "S", SMILE's "Small") *only when combined with a quality filter*. Small caps without a quality screen historically underperform.
- **India.** Indian factor evidence exists but was not researched here (e.g. IIM Ahmedabad's Indian Fama-French factor data library and NSE's Nifty quality/value indices). The authors of the US papers report international robustness for QMJ (24 countries), but specific Indian results were not verified.

### Gaps
- The Fama-French papers were not fetched this session, and the exact factor premia and t-stats were not quoted.
- No Indian-market academic study on QMJ or profitability was retrieved. The IIMA factor library (Agarwalla, Jacob & Varma) is a suggested follow-up.
- Post-2015 factor performance was not covered: value had a weak 2010s and partly recovered from 2021 to 2022. Factor returns are cyclical and have shrunk since publication.
