## Case studies

### Satyam Computer fraud (Jan 2009)
`case_study:satyam-2009` | 2009-01-07 | tickers: US-SAY | confidence: verified

On 7 Jan 2009 chairman B. Ramalinga Raju confessed that Rs 5,040 cr of the Rs 5,361 cr cash and bank balance at Sep-2008 did not exist (total manipulation ~Rs 7,000 cr incl. fake interest accrual, understated liabilities, overstated debtors). Stock: 52-week high ~Rs 544 (2008) -> Rs 179 (6 Jan 2009) -> Rs 39.95 close on 7 Jan (-78%) -> ~Rs 6-7 intraday within days; Tech Mahindra bought control in Apr 2009 at Rs 58/share. Visible-in-advance flags: (1) a huge cash pile earning low interest (other income far below what Rs 5,000 cr+ should yield) while the company did not pay large dividends or buy back stock; (2) debtor days above peers; (3) promoter holding fell from ~25% (2001) to ~8.6% (2008) and much of it was pledged; (4) 16 Dec 2008 board approved a $1.6 bn purchase of promoter-family Maytas Properties/Infra (unrelated real estate) -- reversed in 12 hours after investor revolt; (5) World Bank debarred Satyam in Dec 2008; (6) independent directors resigned in late Dec 2008. Auditor PwC signed off for years (later penalised by SEBI/US PCAOB). Who spotted it: institutional investors who revolted against Maytas; some analysts flagged the unexplained cash yield. Lesson: test that cash earns market interest (interest income / average cash), compare promoter holding trend and pledges, and treat any related-party acquisition proposal by a cash-rich company as a disqualifier.

**Lesson:** Fake cash shows up as low other income; related-party deals by cash-rich firms are a disqualifier
**Spotted early by:** institutional investors (Maytas revolt), forensic-minded analysts

### DHFL collapse: ALM mismatch + promoter-linked loans (2018-2021)
`case_study:dhfl-2019` | 2019-06-04 | tickers: DHFL | confidence: verified

Dewan Housing Finance peaked near Rs 690 in Sep 2018. On 21 Sep 2018, right after IL&FS defaulted, DSP MF sold DHFL commercial paper at a steep yield and the stock fell up to ~60% intraday. Jan 2019: Cobrapost alleged ~Rs 31,000 cr routed to shell companies linked to the Wadhawan promoters; the stock fell 23% in three sessions (Rs 209 to Rs 162). Jun 2019 the company missed bond/CP payments; Nov 2019 RBI took over the board and made it the first NBFC under IBC; Piramal acquired it in 2021 (~Rs 34,250 cr plan) and listed equity was effectively wiped. Visible flags: (1) asset-liability mismatch: 15-20-year home and builder loans funded by short CP and MF money; (2) fast AUM growth into wholesale/developer loans, many to little-known entities with promoter links (Wadhawans also controlled HDIL, the borrower that later sank PMC Bank); (3) thin spreads and leverage ~10x+; (4) heavy MF ownership of its CP = run risk; (5) an auditor's 'no indication' rebuttal of Cobrapost in Mar 2019 produced an 11% bounce that turned out to be a bull trap. Lesson: for lenders, the funding structure (tenor of liabilities vs assets) and borrower concentration matter more than reported NPAs; a sharp rise in CP spreads is a market signal before ratings move.

**Lesson:** Check ALM buckets and wholesale-book borrowers; market spreads lead ratings
**Spotted early by:** Cobrapost investigation; debt-market sellers (DSP MF)

### IL&FS default: AAA-rated group that couldn't pay (2018)
`case_study:ilfs-2018` | 2018-09-04 | tickers: ILFSENGG, ILFSTRANS | confidence: verified

Infrastructure Leasing & Financial Services (owned by LIC, SBI, ORIX, ADIA, HDFC) had ~Rs 91,000 cr group debt across 300+ subsidiaries by Mar 2018 and AAA ratings on some paper until early Aug 2018. First default came at subsidiary IL&FS Transportation (ITNL) in Jun 2018 (~Rs 450 cr ICDs/CP); ICRA cut AAA to AA+ in Jul; the parent defaulted on a Rs 1,000 cr SIDBI loan on 4 Sep 2018; within days ratings went AAA -> D. The government superseded the board on 1 Oct 2018 (Uday Kotak-led). Listed subsidiaries ITNL and IL&FS Engineering fell >90%. The default froze India's CP market and triggered the NBFC crisis (DHFL, Reliance Capital, Altico). Visible flags: (1) consolidated interest coverage near/below 1x -- operating cash flow did not cover interest; (2) long-gestation road/power projects funded with short debt and constant refinancing; (3) 300+ subsidiaries/cross-guarantees made consolidated leverage opaque; (4) ratings leaned on 'parentage' (LIC/SBI) not standalone cash; (5) listed ITNL showed rising debt, receivables from government authorities and claims-based 'income'. Lesson: AAA is not a cash-flow test; a holding structure that needs perpetual refinancing fails the day markets close; rating agency downgrades are lagging.

**Lesson:** Compute interest cover and refinancing needs yourself; parentage-based ratings lag
**Spotted early by:** few; ICRA July 2018 note was late

### Yes Bank: hidden NPAs and RBI moratorium (2018-2020)
`case_study:yes-bank-2020` | 2020-03-05 | tickers: YESBANK | confidence: verified

Yes Bank peaked near Rs 393-404 in Aug 2018 and hit ~Rs 5.6 on 6 Mar 2020 (-98%) after RBI superseded the board and imposed a moratorium (withdrawals capped at Rs 50,000) on 5 Mar 2020. An SBI-led consortium recapitalised it at Rs 10/share; Rs 8,415 cr of AT1 bonds were written off; ~75% of existing shares were locked in for 3 years. Visible flags well before: (1) RBI-assessed NPA divergence: ~Rs 4,177 cr in FY16 and ~Rs 6,355 cr in FY17 (reported NPAs were a fraction of RBI's); (2) loan book growing 30-35%/yr with corporate concentration in stressed groups (ADAG, Essel, DHFL, IL&FS, Cox & Kings, Jet, CG Power); (3) low CASA and dependence on bulk/high-cost deposits; (4) Sep 2018 RBI refused to extend founder Rana Kapoor's term -- a regulator vote of no confidence; (5) promoter shares pledged and sold by lenders; (6) independent director exit in Jan 2019; GNPA jumped from 1.3% (FY18) to 3.2% (FY19) to 7.4% (Sep 2019). Who spotted it: RBI inspections (divergence disclosures were public from 2017), some short-side analysts. Lesson: for banks, divergence disclosures, concentration to stressed groups and regulator actions on management are disqualifiers; 'cheap' P/B after the first fall is a value trap.

**Lesson:** Divergence = understated NPAs; regulator acting on CEO is a sell signal
**Spotted early by:** RBI asset-quality reviews

### Jet Airways grounding (Apr 2019) and liquidation
`case_study:jet-airways-2019` | 2019-04-17 | tickers: JETAIRWAYS | confidence: verified

Jet Airways suspended all flights on 17 Apr 2019 after lenders refused ~Rs 400 cr emergency funding; Indian Oil had stopped fuel on 5 Apr. Net debt ~$1.2 bn; fleet fell from 123 to 41 planes by Mar 2019. The airline posted losses in 8 of the 10 years to 2019; domestic share fell from ~22.5% (2015) to ~15.5% (2018). Etihad bought 24% for $379 m in 2013 -- a rescue mistaken by many as validation. The stock (IPO 2005 at Rs 1,100; peaked >Rs 1,000 in 2006 and again ~Rs 900 in Jan 2018) slid to ~Rs 30s after grounding; a Jalan-Kalrock revival failed and the Supreme Court ordered liquidation in Nov 2024. Visible flags: (1) negative net worth for years; (2) the overpriced 2007 Sahara (JetLite) acquisition; (3) sale-and-leaseback gains propping profits; (4) Aug 2018: the board deferred Q1 results and salaries were delayed; (5) aircraft lessors grounding planes for non-payment (late 2018); (6) promoter Naresh Goyal refusing to cede control. Lesson: in airlines, cumulative losses + negative equity + delayed results/salaries means equity is an option on a bailout; a strategic investor's entry is not a safety net. Speculative rallies in grounded/insolvent stocks are noise, not recovery.

**Lesson:** Negative equity + delayed results = disqualifier in airlines
**Spotted early by:** lessors and oil marketing companies (stopped credit)

### Kingfisher Airlines / UB group (2005-2016)
`case_study:kingfisher-ub-2012` | 2012-10-20 | tickers: UBHOLDINGS | confidence: verified

Kingfisher Airlines launched in 2005 and never posted an annual profit. It bought low-cost Air Deccan in 2007 at a premium, mixing a full-service and a low-cost model. By Mar 2013 accumulated losses were ~Rs 16,023 cr and net worth ~-Rs 12,920 cr. DGCA suspended its licence in Oct 2012; dues to a 17-bank SBI-led consortium were ~Rs 9,000 cr+. Vijay Mallya left India in Mar 2016 and has disputed fraud charges. Visible flags: (1) losses every year plus negative net worth; (2) 2010-11 debt restructuring where banks converted loans to equity at a large premium (~Rs 64.48/share) to market -- lenders throwing good money after bad; (3) the Kingfisher brand was used as collateral at a ~Rs 4,000 cr valuation -- intangible collateral; (4) personal and corporate guarantees across UB Holdings, cross-holdings with United Spirits/United Breweries; (5) salary and tax-deduction (TDS/PF) defaults in 2011-12; (6) promoter pledges in group companies (later United Spirits went to Diageo in 2012-13). Lesson: a premium 'lifestyle' brand cannot rescue an airline with negative equity; when lenders accept brand value as security or convert debt at a premium, equity holders are next in line for losses. Cross-guarantees mean one group company can drag others.

**Lesson:** Intangible collateral + negative equity = terminal
**Spotted early by:** staff (unpaid salaries), aviation analysts

### Unitech: debt-funded land bank + 2G (2008-2020)
`case_study:unitech-2008` | 2008-01-15 | tickers: UNITECH | confidence: verified

Unitech hit Rs 546.80 on the BSE in Jan 2008 (one of India's most valuable realty stocks), fell >90% to ~Rs 30 by Feb 2009 (one day -51% in Oct 2008), ~Rs 21.7 in 2011, ~Rs 7 by Dec 2017 (~-98.7%). Business Standard: Unitech and RCom wiped ~97% of shareholder wealth in 10 years. Visible flags: (1) 2006-07 debt-funded land purchases at cycle-peak prices; consolidated debt ballooned just as the 2008 credit crunch hit; (2) diversification into telecom (Unitech Wireless, 2008 2G licences) -- capital allocation outside the core; later CBI 2G charges against MD Sanjay Chandra (jailed 2011; brothers jailed again 2017 over homebuyer funds); (3) homebuyer advances (customer money) used to fund other projects -- ~Rs 7,800 cr owed to ~16,300 buyers across 61 projects; (4) repeated QIPs/dilution in 2009 to survive; (5) project delays and rising 'inventory' with little cash conversion. In Jan 2020 the Supreme Court allowed the government to take over the board. Lesson: in real estate, track customer-advance usage, debt/equity and land bought with borrowed money at peak; operating cash flow, not land-bank value, keeps a developer alive.

**Lesson:** Leverage + peak-cycle land = ruin; diversification is a red flag

### Ricoh India accounting fraud (2016) -- MNC subsidiary is no shield
`case_study:ricoh-india-2016` | 2016-07-19 | tickers: RICOHINDIA | confidence: verified

Ricoh India (Japanese parent held ~74%) admitted in Jul 2016 that its accounts were falsified; the estimated FY16 loss was Rs 1,123 cr (later Rs 1,118 cr) versus a company with a market cap of only a few thousand crore. A PwC forensic audit found unsupported out-of-books entries of Rs 66.7 cr in Q1FY16 alone that turned a Rs 64 cr loss into a Rs 2.7 cr profit; reports suggest the fraud began in FY14. SEBI barred 7 key managerial personnel in Feb 2018 and ordered a fuller forensic audit; the shares were suspended and the parent later recapitalised via NCLT, leaving minority holders with little. Visible flags: (1) the stock ran from ~Rs 100 to above Rs 1,000 in 2013-15 on a sudden revenue surge (services/'managed document' contracts) -- revenue growth far ahead of the parent and peers; (2) trade receivables and unbilled revenue rose faster than sales; (3) Q2FY16 results were delayed and the statutory auditor (BSR/KPMG) raised concerns; (4) thin free float (~26%) helped the run. Lesson: 'MNC parent' is not a governance guarantee for the Indian subsidiary; receivables growing faster than revenue plus delayed results is a classic pre-blowup combination (Beneish DSRI/TATA flags).

**Lesson:** MNC tag is not a shield; check DSO trend
**Spotted early by:** statutory auditor (refused to sign Q2FY16)

### Cox & Kings: forged foreign-subsidiary books (2019)
`case_study:cox-and-kings-2019` | 2019-06-26 | tickers: COX&KINGS | confidence: single-source

Cox & Kings, a 250-year-old travel brand, defaulted on commercial paper on 26 Jun 2019; the stock fell sharply within two days and went to ~Rs 2-3 from ~Rs 250-400 levels of 2015-18. Lenders' claims ~Rs 5,500 cr in insolvency. A lender-appointed PwC audit found ~Rs 4,367 cr siphoned and books manipulated from Apr 2014 to Jun 2019 to obtain bank credit; foreign subsidiaries (e.g., Prometheon) allegedly forged reports of global audit firms and bank statements, which were then consolidated by Indian auditors. Visible flags: (1) a 'travel' business that should be working-capital-positive carried heavy and rising debt; (2) large receivables and loans/advances despite customers paying in advance; (3) CFO persistently weak relative to reported profit; (4) debt-funded 2011 Holidaybreak (UK) acquisition and complex offshore holding chains; (5) consolidated accounts relying on subsidiaries audited by other firms; (6) high promoter pledges and dependence on CP and Yes Bank lending. Lesson: when a business model implies negative working capital but the balance sheet shows high receivables and debt, the accounts are wrong. Offshore subsidiaries audited by different firms are a soft spot in consolidation.

**Lesson:** Business model should predict the working-capital sign; mismatch = red flag

### Karvy Stock Broking misuse of client securities (2019)
`case_study:karvy-2019` | 2019-11-22 | tickers: - | confidence: verified

Karvy Stock Broking (unlisted, one of India's largest retail brokers) used clients' power-of-attorney to pledge their securities (reported ~Rs 2,300-2,700 cr of client shares, >95,000 clients) with banks/NBFCs for its own loans; a net ~Rs 1,096 cr moved to group company Karvy Realty between Apr 2016 and Oct 2019. NSE's inspection led to a SEBI ex-parte order on 22 Nov 2019 barring new clients; NSDL transferred shares back to ~80,000+ clients; SEBI refused relief to lenders who had taken the pledges. NSE expelled Karvy in 2020 and SEBI later cancelled its registration; the CMD was arrested in 2021. Visible flags: (1) a broker borrowing heavily from banks against 'own' securities that were actually client holdings; (2) group diversion into real estate; (3) mismatch between client ledger balances and demat holdings (only detectable by regulators/clients via CAS). Investor-level signal: securities in your Consolidated Account Statement moving to the broker's pool account or being pledged without your consent. ### Lehman Brothers (2008): leverage and Repo 105

### Beneish M-score: cut-offs, components, base rates
`insight:beneish-m-score` | 2026-11-19 | tickers: - | confidence: verified

Beneish (1999) 8-variable model: M = -4.84 + 0.92*DSRI + 0.528*GMI + 0.404*AQI + 0.892*SGI + 0.115*DEPI - 0.172*SGAI + 4.679*TATA - 0.327*LVGI. DSRI = receivable-days index (Ricoh, Cox & Kings); GMI = gross-margin deterioration; AQI = asset quality (non-current assets ex-PP&E growing = capitalisation); SGI = sales growth (high growth raises pressure); DEPI = slowing depreciation; SGAI = SG&A index; TATA = total accruals to assets ((PAT - CFO)/assets) -- the heaviest weight, i.e., profit not backed by cash; LVGI = leverage index. Cut-offs: M > -1.78 = likely manipulator (the threshold for the 8-variable model per Beneish 1999 / Beneish-Lee-Nichols 2013); < -2.22 is commonly used as 'unlikely' (the 5-variable variant / practitioner convention); grey zone between. Base rates: the FAJ 1999 version reported the model identified roughly half of manipulators before public discovery; the often-quoted '76% detected with 17.5% false positives' is for a specific cut-off/error-cost assumption -- treat as unverified. Cornell students flagged Enron in 1998. Limits: not for banks/NBFCs/insurers; noisy for high-growth firms (SGI); one-year snapshot -- trend matters; it flags earnings management, not fraud proof. Use as a filter to trigger deeper work, never as a sole signal.

### Altman Z-score: distress cut-offs and Indian usage
`insight:altman-z-score` | 2026-11-19 | tickers: - | confidence: single-source

Altman (1968) manufacturing model: Z = 1.2*(WC/TA) + 1.4*(RE/TA) + 3.3*(EBIT/TA) + 0.6*(Mkt cap/Total liabilities) + 1.0*(Sales/TA). Zones: Z > 2.99 safe; 1.81-2.99 grey; < 1.81 distress (some tables use 1.8/3.0). Z' (private firms, book equity) cut-offs ~1.23/2.90. Z'' (non-manufacturers/emerging markets, drops sales/TA): Z'' = 6.56*X1 + 3.26*X2 + 6.72*X3 + 1.05*X4, distress < 1.1, safe > 2.6 (EM version adds +3.25 constant). Altman's original sample classified ~95% of bankrupts correctly one year ahead, with accuracy falling sharply 2+ years out -- it's a near-term distress gauge, not a fraud detector. Indian use: run on consolidated numbers; exclude banks/NBFCs/insurers (use leverage, ALM, divergence instead); market-value term (X4) can mask distress in bubbles (Adani 2022, Nikola) -- check with book-equity Z'. Low Z examples pre-collapse: Jet Airways (negative RE and WC), Kingfisher, Future Retail, Unitech 2008. A low Z with rising pledges or CP dependence is a disqualifier; a low Z in a cyclical at trough can be a turnaround -- require debt reduction evidence.

### Piotroski F-score: separating value from value traps
`insight:piotroski-f-score` | 2026-11-19 | tickers: - | confidence: single-source

Piotroski (2000) 9 binary tests for cheap (high book-to-market) stocks. Profitability: ROA > 0; CFO > 0; ROA improving; CFO > net income (accrual check). Leverage/liquidity: long-term debt/assets falling; current ratio rising; no new equity issued. Efficiency: gross margin rising; asset turnover rising. Scores: 8-9 = strong, 0-2 (often <=3) = weak. Original result: within the top book-to-market quintile (1976-1996 US), buying high-F and shorting low-F earned ~23% a year; most low-F firms were the ones that later delisted or were distressed. Out-of-sample evidence is mixed: European studies find ~10%/yr high-minus-low spreads; some US post-publication and China A-share tests find little or none. Use: as a value-trap filter, not an alpha engine -- a 'cheap' stock with F <= 3, CFO < PAT and new equity issuance (Yes Bank 2019, Credit Suisse 2022, Jet 2018, Future Retail 2019) behaves like a trap. Pair with M-score (manipulation) and Z-score (distress).

### Base rates: how common is corporate fraud and who catches it
`insight:fraud-base-rates` | 2026-11-19 | tickers: - | confidence: verified

Dyck, Morse & Zingales ('How pervasive is corporate fraud?', Review of Accounting Studies 2024) use Arthur Andersen's collapse as a natural experiment: in an average year ~10% of large US public companies commit (alleged) securities fraud (95% CI 7-14%), ~41% misrepresent financial reports (excluding clerical errors), and about two-thirds of frauds go undetected. Fraud destroys ~1.6% of equity value per year (~$830 bn in 2021). An earlier version estimated 15%. Their 2010 Journal of Finance paper ('Who blows the whistle') found detection mostly came from employees, media, analysts and industry regulators, with auditors and the SEC a minority (exact shares unverified here). Implications for a long-term portfolio: (1) with 20-30 holdings, expect 2-3 to have some misreporting at any time -- diversification and position sizing are fraud insurance; (2) audit sign-off is weak evidence; (3) journalists, short sellers, employees and lenders often see it first (Cobrapost/DHFL, FT/Wirecard, Muddy Waters/Luckin, Hindenburg/Nikola, Cooper/WorldCom). In India, add SEBI forensic audits and NSE/BSE surveillance as detectors; SME and small caps likely have higher rates (no reliable estimate).

### Value-trap patterns: why 'cheap' kept getting cheaper
`insight:value-trap-patterns` | 2026-11-19 | tickers: - | confidence: verified

Patterns from Yes Bank, Credit Suisse, GE, Jet Airways, Future Retail, PC Jeweller, Zee, Reliance Capital, Paytm, Evergrande: (1) Low P/B in a lender with understated NPAs -- book value is fictional (Yes Bank divergence; CS at 0.2x TBV); (2) Low P/E on low-quality earnings -- receivable factoring, contract assets, non-GAAP adjustments (GE, Valeant); (3) Dividend yield funded by debt -- dividend > FCF (GE 2017, Evergrande); (4) 'Asset value' that needs a buyer: land banks, brands, airline slots, spectrum (Unitech, Kingfisher brand collateral, RCom spectrum) -- in distress, asset values fall with the company; (5) Promoter-levered good business -- great franchise, bad stock while pledges overhang (Zee); (6) Equity ahead of a restructuring -- debt converts, lenders or IBC wipe equity (Jet, Future, RCap, DHFL); (7) Structural decline mistaken for cyclical trough (print media, legacy telecom); (8) Post-IPO decline in insider-sold story stocks -- 'it's 70% off' is not cheap if it was 5x overpriced (Paytm). Tests: is FCF positive and covering dividends? Is debt falling? Is the 'asset' saleable today at book? Is the promoter unlevered? Piotroski F >= 7 and Z > 2.99 for value buys; else require a dated catalyst. Counter-example: genuine turnarounds (e.g., Suzlon after deleveraging) needed debt actually extinguished, not promised.

### India: promoter pledges and group contagion
`insight:promoter-pledge-group-contagion` | 2026-11-19 | tickers: - | confidence: verified

Indian blow-ups repeatedly ran through promoter-level leverage rather than the listed company's own P&L: Zee/Essel (pledge 59% -> ~90%, promoter stake ~42% -> ~4%), ADAG (near-100% pledges; RCap, RCom, RHFL), Future Group (FRL promoter stake 47% -> 14% via invocations), Satyam (promoter stake ~25% -> ~8.6%, pledged), Kingfisher/UB (cross-guarantees), Cox & Kings. Mechanism: falling price -> margin call -> lenders invoke and dump shares -> further fall (reflexive), often triggered by a credit event elsewhere (IL&FS Sep 2018 froze MF/NBFC lending against shares). Signals: pledge % of promoter holding (disclosed quarterly under SAST; 'encumbrance' incl. non-disposal undertakings); rising pledge while price rises = borrowing against inflated value; listed company guarantees, letters of comfort, FDs or ICDs to group entities; MF/NBFC 'loan against shares' exposure to the group; promoter selling via 'gifts' or off-market transfers (PC Jeweller). Rules: pledge >25% of promoter holding or any rising trend = disqualifier for long-term holding; check all group listed companies, not just the one you own -- contagion is group-wide (Essel, ADAG, Future, IL&FS). SEBI disclosure since 2019 includes encumbrance reasons; track quarterly.

### Banks/NBFCs: blow-up signals (M/Z-scores don't apply)
`insight:lender-blowup-signals` | 2026-11-19 | tickers: - | confidence: verified

Financials need their own tests (IL&FS, DHFL, Yes Bank, RCap, Lehman, SVB, Credit Suisse): (1) Funding: share of CP/short-term wholesale funding; ALM gap in the 1-year bucket (DHFL, IL&FS); uninsured/bulk deposit share and depositor concentration (SVB ~90%+, Yes Bank low CASA). (2) Asset quality: RBI divergence disclosures (Yes Bank FY16-17), restructured + SMA-2 book, loans to promoter-group or 'general purpose corporate loans' (RHFL), wholesale/developer concentration. (3) Growth: loan growth >30%/yr for several years in corporate books. (4) Capital: leverage (Lehman ~30x; NBFCs >7-8x), Level 3/model-marked assets vs equity, fair-value of HTM securities vs equity (SVB losses ~= equity). (5) Market: CP/bond spread spikes, CDS (Credit Suisse Oct 2022), rating outlooks; (6) Governance: regulator actions on CEO (Yes Bank), auditor exits (RCap PwC), material weakness opinions (CS), CRO vacancies (SVB). (7) Window-dressing: quarter-end vs average balance sheet (Repo 105). Rule: a lender trading at a discount to book with any two of these is pricing hidden losses -- avoid; equity is a thin slice (10x leverage means a 10% asset loss wipes it).

### Short-seller reports: what usually follows
`insight:short-seller-reports-outcomes` | 2026-11-19 | tickers: - | confidence: verified

Case outcomes: confirmed frauds -- Luckin (Muddy Waters, admitted within ~2 months), Nikola (Hindenburg, founder convicted), Wirecard (FT/Zatarra/shorts, 5 years early; regulator attacked the shorts), Valeant (Citron, -90%), Enron (Chanos), Lehman (Einhorn). Contested -- Adani (Hindenburg 2023: -60% to -70% drawdowns, partial recovery, SEBI Sep 2025 closed RPT allegations without penalty; other matters pending; Hindenburg closed Jan 2025). Practical rules: (1) Evaluate the evidence type -- primary documents, store counts, registries and ex-employee testimony (Luckin, Nikola) are stronger than valuation arguments; (2) Management response quality: point-by-point rebuttal with documents vs attacks on the messenger (Wirecard, Valeant called reports 'manipulation'); (3) Independent auditor/special audit outcome (KPMG at Wirecard could not verify); (4) Even when not fraud, fragile balance sheets and stretched valuations produce big drawdowns (Adani); (5) Regulators defending a 'national champion' is not evidence. Base rates: academic studies of activist short reports generally find large negative announcement returns that persist on average (exact magnitudes vary; unverified here). For a long-only holder: on a credible report, cut to a size you can hold through a 60% fall, then wait for independent verification.

### Auditor resignation / delayed accounts as a disqualifier
`insight:auditor-exit-signal` | 2026-11-19 | tickers: - | confidence: verified

Auditor exits and delayed audited results preceded or accompanied many collapses: Reliance Capital & Reliance Home Finance (PwC resigned 2019 citing unresolved concerns), Byju's (Deloitte Jun 2023, BDO Sep 2024; FY21 accounts ~18 months late), Ricoh India (Q2FY16 results delayed after auditor queries), Jet Airways (Q1FY19 results deferred Aug 2018), Wirecard (KPMG special audit could not verify; EY refused 2019 sign-off), Credit Suisse (material weaknesses Mar 2023), Gensol/Brightcom (already in memory). In India, after the 2018-19 wave of mid-term auditor resignations, SEBI (Oct 2019) required auditors who resign to complete the quarter's limited review and disclose detailed reasons, which are filed on exchanges -- read them. Rules: (1) mid-term resignation with stated concerns (information not provided, fund diversion, going concern) = exit; (2) 'pre-occupation' or fee-related reasons from a large firm replaced by a tiny firm = strong flag; (3) delayed results beyond the regulatory deadline, or repeated qualified opinions/emphasis of matter on related-party balances = flag; (4) CFO resignation within months of auditor change compounds the signal. Auditor sign-off is weak evidence of honesty (Satyam/PwC, Wirecard/EY), but auditor exit is strong evidence of trouble.
