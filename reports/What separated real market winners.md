# Winners Had Edges the Books Leave Out

You are right that something is missing, and it is not a better checklist. Each winner in your list had at least one advantage that reading cannot supply. Young Buffett had tiny capital in an unscreened market, read about 20,000 pages of Moody's manuals, and later ran Berkshire on about 1.7x leverage that cost less than T-bills. Lynch had a fund that started at about $20 million, worked roughly 13-hour days with 200+ company visits a year, and had access to management before Regulation FD existed. Damani and Jhunjhunwala grew up inside a small, lightly policed Dalal Street, made their early money trading (much of it short in 1992, some of it with borrowed money), and then let one asset carry them. For Damani that asset was **D-Mart, a business he founded**. For Jhunjhunwala it was **Titan, about a third of his portfolio at his death**. Porinju Veliyath's real edge is early discovery in small caps, and the cost is brutal: his PMS lost about two-thirds of its value in FY19–FY20. P R Sundar's most verifiable income was **₹4.6 crore of advisory fees that SEBI ruled unregistered**, not audited trading profits. Jane Street and India's prop desks earn from the structure of the market itself: speed, scale, balance sheet and the other side of retail's option buying. **Individuals lost ₹72,243 crore gross in F&O in FY26, while prop traders made ₹44,483 crore and FPIs ₹13,896 crore**, and 99% of that institutional profit went to algorithmic firms. Underneath every story are the same non-knowledge variables: time, starting capital, savings, behavior under drawdowns, never being forced to sell, and luck, which dominates any record shorter than a decade. Most very large fortunes come from owning a business rather than picking stocks. For an Indian individual the realistic edges are narrow but real: domain knowledge, small and neglected companies, a horizon longer than institutions can afford, staying out of F&O, a high savings rate, and owning a business. This report is educational and is not personalised financial advice.

## Each legend's edge was structural, temporary and mostly unavailable to you

The table below sets out what the research found for each name in your list. In almost every case the advantage came from circumstance (era, capital size, network, structure) as much as from insight, and much of it has since been competed away or regulated out.

| Winner | The real edge | Capital and leverage structure | Can you replicate it today? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Young Buffett (1950–69) | Exhaustive reading of obscure securities. Field checks. Tiny capital in an unscreened market | ~$9,800 personal savings, then $105,100 partnership with no management fee | The work ethic, yes. The 1950s net-net universe, no: screens now find them in seconds |
| Berkshire Buffett (1970s–) | Cheap, safe, quality stocks held for decades | ~1.7x leverage via insurance float costing ~3 points below T-bills | No. Broker margin is costly and callable |
| Peter Lynch (1977–90) | Industrial-scale legwork, pre-Reg FD management access, small starting fund | ~$20M fund, closed to new money until 1981 | Partly: the legwork on a handful of companies |
| R K Damani | Broker-family network, 1992 bear trades, then founding D-Mart | Founder stake of ~67–82% in Avenue Supermarts | The business-building lesson, yes. The network and era, no |
| Rakesh Jhunjhunwala | Trading skill, borrowed early capital, being bearish in 1992, then one huge concentrated holding (Titan) | ₹5,000 seed plus money from his brother's clients | The holding-through-pessimism lesson only |
| Porinju Veliyath | Early discovery of neglected small caps. His own following moves prices | Fee-earning PMS (2% fixed plus 10% over a 10% hurdle) | Partly, if you can survive a −67% two-year drawdown |
| P R Sundar | Option-selling style plus a large audience | Most verifiable income was advisory fees | No. Retail loss rates in options are about 88–93% |
| Jane Street and prop desks | Speed, scale, balance sheet, order-flow visibility | Billions in risk capital, member-level costs | No |

### Buffett got rich twice, and the second time was through leverage and longevity

The young Buffett was a hustler before he was an investor. Newspaper routes, golf balls and pinball machines produced about **$9,800 in savings** by the time he finished school ([Saber Capital](https://sabercapitalmgt.com/buffetts-early-investments/)). His research edge was volume. He reportedly went through about **20,000 pages of Moody's manuals, twice**, and in 1952 found Western Insurance Securities trading at roughly 1x earnings ([GuruFocus](https://www.gurufocus.com/news/1149512/some-thoughts-on-warren-buffetts-early-investments-in-western-insurance-services)). In 1951 he put about 65% of his net worth into GEICO and returned about **75.8% against the Dow's 21.3%**. The widely repeated "144%" figure has no source ([GuruFocus](https://www.gurufocus.com/news/248721/how-buffett-crushed-the-dow-in-1951-lessons-for-investing-with-small-sums)). He then apprenticed under Graham at Graham-Newman for $12,000 a year, inside the deal flow of the best value shop in America ([Financial History](https://fhmagazine.org/financial-history-134-summer-2020/0850549001596540706/p15)).

The partnership started in 1956 with **$105,100 from family and friends**. It charged no management fee and took 25% of profits above a 6% hurdle. Limited partners compounded at about **23.8% a year net against 7.4% for the Dow** ([Rational Walk](https://rationalwalk.com/highlights-from-buffett-partnership-letters/)). The trades were concentrated and hands-on. About a quarter of partnership assets went into Sanborn Map, whose investment portfolio was worth more than its share price, and Buffett took a board seat to force the value out ([GuruFocus, 1960 letter](https://www.gurufocus.com/news/7227/warren-buffett-partnership-letter-of-1960)). At Dempster Mill he took 70% control and brought in a turnaround manager ([MOI Global](https://moiglobal.com/warren-buffetts-ground-rules/)). During the 1963 salad-oil scandal he checked whether Omaha restaurants and banks still took American Express, and reportedly built a stake of about 40% of the partnership's assets ([The Grain Desk](https://thegraindesk.substack.com/p/main-street-still-trusted-the-card)). That 40% figure has not been verified against his letters. Buffett himself says small size was the edge: "it's a huge structural advantage not to have a lot of money," and he claimed he could make 50% a year on $1 million ([AOL/Moneywise](https://www.aol.com/finance/warren-buffett-certain-could-earn-121300309.html)).

The fortune came later, and from something else. AQR's "Buffett's Alpha" paper found Berkshire ran about **1.7-to-1 leverage**, funded by insurance float that cost an average **1.72% a year, about 3 points below T-bills**, with AAA-rated debt. Its Sharpe ratio was **0.79**, which the authors call very good but not superhuman. His alpha became statistically insignificant once exposure to cheap, safe, quality stocks was controlled for ([Frazzini, Kabiller & Pedersen, FAJ 2018](https://gwern.net/doc/economics/2018-frazzini.pdf)). Their verdict is the single most useful sentence in this report. His genius lay in "recognizing early on that these investment themes work, applying leverage without ever having a fire sale, and sticking to his principles." An individual cannot buy leverage that is cheaper than the government's and can never be called. Morgan Housel's arithmetic shows why time mattered even more: **about 99% of Buffett's net worth came after age 65**. Had he started at 30 with $25,000, earned 22% a year and stopped at 60, he would have had about $11.9 million ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2024/05/03/most-of-warren-buffetts-wealth-came-after-age-65-heres-why.html); [CSQ](https://csq.com/2020/08/how-warren-buffet-amassed-his-fortune/)).

His failures share a pattern: fading businesses bought because they looked cheap. He called Berkshire's textile mill the "dumbest" stock he ever bought and a **"$200 billion" mistake** in opportunity cost ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/id/39724884)). Dexter Shoe, bought for $433 million in Berkshire stock, was "the worst deal that I've made" ([Business Insider](https://www.businessinsider.nl/warren-buffetts-most-gruesome-mistake-was-buying-dexter-shoe-with-berkshire-hathaway-stock-heres-a-look-back-at-his-12-billion-error)). In 2020 he dumped all four US airlines at a loss and said "my mistake" ([Kiplinger](https://www.kiplinger.com/article/investing/t052-c008-s001-buffett-dumps-airline-stocks-cheerleads-america.html)).

### Lynch out-worked everyone during a 13-year window, then quit

Lynch caddied for Fidelity's president as a teenager, bought Flying Tiger in college for his first tenbagger, and joined Fidelity as an analyst ([PBS Frontline](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/pages/frontline/shows/betting/pros/lynch.html)). Magellan was about **$20 million when he took over in 1977 and $14 billion when he left** ([PBS Frontline](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/pages/frontline/shows/betting/pros/lynch.html)). It returned about **29.2% a year against the market's 15.5%** on CRSP data ([CapexCat](https://capexcat.substack.com/p/peter-lynch-buy-what-you-know)), or 26.4% against 13.3% on CBS's measure ([CBS MoneyWatch](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/lessons-from-a-great-fund-managers-record)). The best years came while the fund was small and closed to new money, until 1981.

The workload is the part nobody copies. Lynch arrived around 6:45 a.m., stayed about 13 hours, worked Saturdays, spent a week a month on the road, and made **200+ company visits while reading about 700 annual reports a year**. First-year turnover was 343%, and the fund eventually held about 1,400 stocks ([CapexCat](https://capexcat.substack.com/p/peter-lynch-buy-what-you-know)). In his own words: "I was working six days a week and that wasn't even enough… I could see any company I wanted to see" ([PBS Frontline](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/pages/frontline/shows/betting/pros/lynch.html)). That open access to management existed before Regulation FD curbed selective disclosure in 2000. His biggest bets were unpopular. He held Chrysler near bankruptcy as his largest position while people asked "Don't you know they're going bankrupt?" He also credited luck openly. He retired at 46 because "it was just too much time." After he left, Magellan at $14–50 billion returned 7.8% a year and trailed the S&P ([CBS MoneyWatch](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/lessons-from-a-great-fund-managers-record)). "Buy what you know" is a distortion. He says he never told people to invest on that basis, and his fuller line is "know what you own" ([Yahoo Finance](https://ca.finance.yahoo.com/news/fidelity-legend-peter-lynch-i-never-said-to-invest-in-the-stock-market-120755736.html)).

## India's legends were traders and founders first, stock pickers second

### Damani's fortune is a supermarket, not a portfolio

Radhakishan Damani came from a broking family. His father was on Dalal Street, and after his father's death he left a ball-bearings business to join his brother's broking firm ([Business Today](https://www.businesstoday.in/markets/stocks/rakesh-jhunjunwala-guru-radhakishan-damani-second-richest-mukesh-ambani/story/396422.html)). That background was the information edge in a small ring-trading market. In the early 1990s he was short while Harshad Mehta pushed prices up, and later he shorted stocks Mehta promoted "until they fell" ([Rediff](https://www.rediff.com/business/special/how-well-do-you-know-d-marts-damani/20170405.htm)). Folklore says the short nearly bankrupted him before the scam broke and links him to Manu Manek's "bear cartel". Neither claim has a primary source, so treat both as legend. He then moved to long holdings: HDFC Bank at its 1995 listing, VST, Gillette and Crisil. His line on paying up for quality was that you can't live on Peddar Road at Dharavi's rates ([Rediff](https://www.rediff.com/business/special/how-well-do-you-know-d-marts-damani/20170405.htm)).

The decisive move was leaving the market around 2000 to build D-Mart. He owned his stores rather than leasing them, priced everyday groceries about 8% below retail, turned inventory 3–4x faster than peers and paid vendors in about 10 days. He expanded slowly: about 10 stores when Big Bazaar had 250 ([Outlook Business](https://www.outlookbusiness.com/lead-story/radhakishan-damani-the-slow-and-steady-retail-king-6675)). At the 2017 IPO his **~82% stake was worth over ₹32,000 crore, about 90% of his net worth** ([Rediff](https://www.rediff.com/business/special/how-well-do-you-know-d-marts-damani/20170405.htm)). His roughly $14–16 billion today rises and falls with D-Mart's valuation ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radhakishan_Damani)). The honest lesson from Damani is about running a business on cost discipline and owned assets. It is not about stock-picking.

### Jhunjhunwala's "₹5,000" was seed money, and the real fuel was leverage, 1992 and Titan

Jhunjhunwala qualified as a chartered accountant and **started with ₹5,000 in 1985**. His father refused to fund him ([ThePrint](https://theprint.in/theprint-profile/rakesh-jhunjhunwala-was-investor-with-midas-touch-who-never-gave-up-on-indian-markets/1654819)). He then borrowed from his brother's clients, promising them more than fixed-deposit returns ([ThePrint](https://theprint.in/india/indias-warren-buffett-the-man-with-the-midas-touch-who-was-rakesh-jhunjhunwala/1082239)). His famous 1986 trade was **5,000 Tata Tea shares at ₹43**, which reached ₹143 in about three months ([ThePrint](https://theprint.in/theprint-profile/rakesh-jhunjhunwala-was-investor-with-midas-touch-who-never-gave-up-on-indian-markets/1654819)). That position cost about ₹2.15 lakh, more than 40 times his seed capital, so it was funded with other people's money. He said he made "my biggest wealth" in 1991–92 by being bearish during the Mehta scam ([ThePrint](https://theprint.in/india/indias-warren-buffett-the-man-with-the-midas-touch-who-was-rakesh-jhunjhunwala/1082239)). He described himself plainly: "I love trading more than investing… I never trade my investments," and "you can't make money on borrowed knowledge" ([Outlook Business](https://www.outlookbusiness.com/interview/you-cant-make-money-on-borrowed-knowledge-566)).

Titan made the legend. By his own account he bought around **₹30 in October 2001**, and he held through volatility because "there was no permanent impairment of value" ([Outlook Business](https://www.outlookbusiness.com/interview/you-cant-make-money-on-borrowed-knowledge-566)). Filings show 664,250 shares bought between March and June 2002 at around ₹50 unadjusted ([Open Magazine](https://openthemagazine.com/features/living/jhunjhunwalas-real-bull-run)). The "₹3" figure is the split- and bonus-adjusted price ([Business Today](https://www.businesstoday.in/markets/top-story/story/80000-return-in-20-years-atul-suri-explains-how-mentor-rakesh-jhunjunwala-spotted-titan-stock-346458-2022-09-06)). At his death in August 2022, his 5.1% Titan stake was worth **₹11,087 crore, about a third of a 32-stock portfolio** ([ThePrint](https://theprint.in/india/indias-warren-buffett-the-man-with-the-midas-touch-who-was-rakesh-jhunjhunwala/1082239); [BNN Bloomberg](https://www.bnnbloomberg.ca/indian-billionaire-s-stock-holdings-worth-nearly-4-billion-in-focus-after-death-1.1806140.amp.html)). Take Titan out and his record is still strong, but it is no longer extraordinary.

The failures are instructive. He bought 21% of A2Z Maintenance for about ₹20 crore and kept adding, including after listing. The stock fell 94% as debt went from ₹405 crore to more than ₹1,100 crore ([Outlook Business](https://www.outlookbusiness.com/markets/trend/spectacular-bust-321)). He admitted he "misjudged the quality and character of the entrepreneur" in A2Z and Bilcare ([Outlook Business](https://www.outlookbusiness.com/interview/you-cant-make-money-on-borrowed-knowledge-566)). In 2021 he, his wife and others settled SEBI's Aptech insider-trading case without admitting or denying the charges. SEBI alleged he held unpublished price-sensitive information about Aptech's preschool foray and passed it to relatives who traded. **He personally paid about ₹18.5 crore**, made up of ₹9.51 crore settlement, ₹5.87 crore disgorgement and ₹3.11 crore interest ([SEBI order](https://www.sebi.gov.in/sebi_data/attachdocs/jul-2021/1626425517810.pdf)). Being close to promoters was part of the edge, and in that case SEBI alleged it crossed the line.

### Porinju's record is real, and so is the two-thirds drawdown

Porinju Veliyath started as a floor trader at Kotak in 1990, worked as an analyst at Parag Parikh Securities, and founded Equity Intelligence in Kochi in 2002 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porinju_Veliyath)). He runs concentrated portfolios of 10–12 mostly small and mid-cap stocks and charges a **2% fixed fee plus 10% of returns above a 10% hurdle** ([Equity Intelligence](https://www.equityintelligence.com/portfolio-management-service)). The firm's own numbers show both sides. FY14 returned +59%, FY15 +79% and FY21 +152%. **FY19 was −31.87% and FY20 −51.48%**, while the Nifty 50 TRI was +16.45% and −25.02% ([Equity Intelligence](https://www.equityintelligence.com/portfolio-management-service)). Compounded, those two years come to about −67%. An investor who entered at the FY18 peak was back to roughly breakeven only after FY22, four years in which the Nifty nearly doubled. The causes were partly his own. He publicly backed low-governance "chor" stocks for re-rating and later admitted the strategy "backfired" ([blog compilation of ET interviews](https://rakesh-jhunjhunwala.in/i-burnt-fingers-badly-with-chor-stocks-porinju-veliyath-concedes-defeat-vows-to-stay-away-from-chors-invest-only-in-cos-with-integrity/)). He held about 8% of LEEL Electricals, which collapsed. He alleged diversion of sale proceeds by the promoters and said "in hindsight it looks a mistake" ([Hans India](https://www.thehansindia.com/posts/index/Latest-News/2019-01-13/Made-a-mistake-says-Porinju-Veliyath-after-investor-wealth-eroded-by-44/474694)).

The "Porinju effect" is the trap for copycats. His bulk-deal buys often trigger upper circuits as retail investors pile in. BEML, for example, rose 51% in five weeks after his firm bought a stake ([Business Standard](https://www.business-standard.com/topic/porinju-veliyath)). Followers buy after the disclosure and the move, at worse prices, without his information or his exit timing. The only SEBI order naming him that this research found is a 2009 consent order in a Geojit Securities matter, whose terms could not be read ([SEBI](https://www.sebi.gov.in/enforcement/orders/sep-2009/consent-order-on-the-application-submitted-by-shri-porinju-veliyath-in-the-matter-of-geojit-securities-limited_3827.html)). No front-running or manipulation finding was found. There is also a business-model point: a 2% fixed fee keeps accruing in years when clients lose two-thirds.

### P R Sundar: the 2023 SEBI order, not 2024, and income from fees

Sundar's fame rests on selling Nifty, Bank Nifty and stock options to collect time decay. His audience is about 1.2 million YouTube subscribers, and he sells paid workshops ([ISFM](https://isfm.co.in/p-r-sundar-net-worth/)). His trading returns are entirely self-reported. He calls 25–30% a year "very good" ([X](https://x.com/PRSundar64/status/1696158361501261954)), and his own tweet shows the tail risk of the strategy: "Lost 15 lakhs as I sold Call options yesterday" ([X](https://twitter.com/PRSundar64/status/1215213725033984000)).

The verifiable money is in SEBI's **settlement order of 25 May 2023**, not 2024. SEBI found that his company, Mansun Consultancy, sold "daily calls on Telegram" through his blog without investment-adviser registration and collected **₹4,59,95,763** in fees. Sundar, his wife and the company settled without admitting or denying the findings. They paid **₹6,07,69,863 in disgorgement** (fees plus 12% interest) and ₹46.8 lakh in settlement amounts, and accepted a one-year bar from dealing in securities ([SEBI order](https://www.sebi.gov.in/sebi_data/attachdocs/may-2023/1685095933750.pdf)). Media reports of a "₹6.5 crore penalty" round these figures together. SEBI has since gone after the same pattern of "education" that is really advice. It ordered "Baap of Chart" to refund ₹17 crore ([BQ Prime](https://www.bqprime.com/law-and-policy/sebi-bars-baap-of-chart-finfluencer-orders-rs-17-crore-refund)), and in December 2025 impounded more than ₹546 crore from Avadhut Sathe's trading academy ([JusCorpus](https://www.juscorpus.com/educators-or-unlicensed-advisers/)). For a famous market personality, the dependable income is fees from followers. Trading profits are optional and unaudited.

## Jane Street and the prop desks profit from the side of the trade retail takes

FIIs and prop firms are not smarter readers of the same books. They are built differently, and in Indian F&O the difference decides the outcome. SEBI's August 2026 study of FY25–FY26 found individuals lost **₹97,882 crore gross in FY25 and ₹72,243 crore in FY26**. In the same years prop traders made ₹45,955 crore and ₹44,483 crore, and FPIs ₹31,085 crore and ₹13,896 crore. **99% of FPI and prop gross profit went to entities using algorithms**, and the **top 10 prop entities took about 75% of the prop category's profit**. Options produced 92% of individual losses and about 98% of prop profit. In FY25, 70% of index-options turnover happened on expiry day ([SEBI, Aug 2026](https://www.sebi.gov.in/sebi_data/attachdocs/aug-2026/1787233506209.pdf)). Individuals also paid about **₹25,000 crore a year in transaction costs**, roughly ₹1 lakh crore over FY22–FY26, which is a certain loss before any skill enters ([SEBI, Aug 2026](https://www.sebi.gov.in/sebi_data/attachdocs/aug-2026/1787233506209.pdf)). The losses fall on the most fragile traders. In FY26, 43% of traders were under 30 and 89% of them lost money. **The 13% of traders with portfolios under ₹1 lakh but turnover above ₹1 crore accounted for 52% of all losses** ([SEBI, Aug 2026](https://www.sebi.gov.in/sebi_data/attachdocs/aug-2026/1787233506209.pdf)).

The professional business model has four parts. Firms collect the bid-ask spread on enormous volume. They sell short-dated options that retail buys, earning the premium of implied over realised volatility and the "lottery premium" on far out-of-the-money strikes. They arbitrage the index against its constituents and futures against options. And they win speed races. On London Stock Exchange data, latency races occur about once a minute per FTSE 100 stock and last 5–10 microseconds. The top six firms win or lose more than 80% of them ([FCA Occasional Paper 50](https://www.fca.org.uk/publications/occasional-papers/occasional-paper-no-50-quantifying-high-frequency-trading-arms-race-new-methodology)). The scale is enormous. Jane Street posted **$39.6 billion of net trading revenue in 2025**, more than JPMorgan's trading unit ([Hedgeweek](https://www.hedgeweek.com/jane-street-outpaces-rivals-with-record-40bn-trading-haul/)). Citadel Securities' India arm earned about ₹4,060 crore of revenue in the year to March 2025 with a team of about ten ([Samosa Capital](https://samosacapital.beehiiv.com/p/citadel-s-india-profits-surge-even-as-regulators-rein-in-trading-daily-india-briefing)). India accounts for about 60% of global equity-derivatives contract volume ([FIA](https://www.fia.org/marketvoice/articles/explainer-meteoric-rise-indias-equity-derivatives-volume)). Global trading firms are not opening offices in Mumbai for the fundamentals.

The Jane Street case shows what scale makes possible. SEBI's interim order of 3 July 2025 alleged that the group bought Bank Nifty constituent stocks and futures in the morning to lift the index while building larger options positions on the other side, then sold the cash and futures later in the day. It also alleged the group pushed settlement levels in the last hour of expiry ([Mondaq](https://www.mondaq.com/india/securities/1648508/sebi-update-%7c-interim-order-against-jane-street-group-for-alleged-index-manipulation); [Wright Research](https://www.wrightresearch.in/blog/why-did-sebi-ban-jane-street-from-indian-markets/)). Between January 2023 and March 2025 the group made **₹36,502 crore net**. It earned about ₹43,289 crore on options and lost money on stock futures and cash, which is the alleged lever-and-payload structure ([Republic World](https://www.republicworld.com/business/sebi-banned-jane-street-after-rs-36500-cr-windfall-in-india-heres-what-you-need-to-know)). SEBI impounded **₹4,843.5 crore**, the largest such order in its history ([Business Standard](https://www.business-standard.com/amp/markets/news/sebi-bars-jane-street-orders-rs-4844-cr-disgorgement-market-manipulation-125070400081_1.html)). Jane Street disputes the findings. Trading resumed after the escrow deposit, and as of November 2026 there is no final ruling: SAT reserved its order on a document-disclosure plea on 21 October 2026 ([LiveLaw](https://www.livelawbiz.com/securities-law/sat/securities-appellate-tribunal-reserves-order-jane-street-challenge-sebi-non-disclosure-relevant-documents-555493)). Whatever the final verdict, the economics are not in dispute. Only a firm with the balance sheet to move index constituents could even attempt that trade. The likely counterparties in aggregate were retail option buyers on expiry day.

Long-only FIIs work differently again. Their India decisions are top-down and relative: index weight, dollar returns after currency moves, and valuation against other emerging markets. India's MSCI EM weight fell from about 20% to **11.94% by April 2026** as Taiwan and Korea rallied ([Finnovate](https://www.finnovate.in/learn/blog/msci-em-india-weight-fpi-flows-taiwan-korea)). FPIs sold ₹1,25,736 crore net in FY26 while domestic institutions absorbed a record ₹8.5 lakh crore, fed by SIPs ([SEBI Bulletin](https://www.sebi.gov.in/sebi_data/attachdocs/apr-2026/1777539656528.pdf)). Reading FII selling as a verdict on a company is usually a mistake. It is mostly allocation. Size also handicaps big funds. About 79% of Indian mid- and small-cap funds trailed their benchmark over the ten years to 2025 ([Angel One summary of SPIVA](https://www.angelone.in/news/mutual-funds/most-indian-equity-funds-underperform-s-p-indices-over-10-years)).

## Knowing the rules is common; time, capital, temperament and survival are scarce

### The rich lists are mostly founders, and the dead copycats are invisible

Kaplan and Rauh found that **69% of the 2011 Forbes 400 started their own business**, up from 40% in 1982 ([Stanford GSB](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/joshua-rauh-what-forbes-400-list-says-about-american-wealth)). The "finance" fortunes on those lists mostly belong to fund *managers* collecting fees on other people's capital. That is a business, not personal stock-picking ([MPIfG paper](https://pure.mpg.de/pubman/item/item_2418682_8/component/file_2632377/SSR_65_2017_Korom.pdf)). No dataset counts Forbes 30 Under 30 honorees who got there by picking stocks. The list is built from founders, which is your answer: under-30 wealth at that scale comes from equity in something you built, because even an elite return on a young person's savings is small in rupees.

Survivorship hides the people who ran the same playbook and lost. **Bessembinder found that about 4% of US listed stocks since 1926 account for all net wealth creation over T-bills**, and most stocks trail T-bills over their lifetimes ([ASU W.P. Carey](https://blogs.wpcarey.asu.edu/20180917-do-stocks-outperform-treasury-bills)). LTCM had Nobel laureates on its board and over 25:1 leverage, and lost about $4.6 billion in under four months ([McGraw-Hill case](https://highered.mheducation.com/sites/dl/free/0077151569/977786/c26i_LTCM.pdf)). Archegos turned about $36 billion of capital into about $160 billion of exposure. Its collapse cost banks over $10 billion, not the "$20 billion" often quoted, and Bill Hwang was sentenced to 18 years ([DOJ](https://www.justice.gov/usao-sdny/pr/founder-and-head-archegos-capital-management-bill-hwang-sentenced-18-years-prison)). In India, Mehta and Ketan Parekh were the leveraged bulls of the same era in which Damani and Jhunjhunwala made their names. The BSE Smallcap fell about **68% in 2008**, and since 2004 the small-cap index has spent 36% of trading days more than 30% below its peak ([Business Standard](https://business-standard.com/article/markets/is-the-worst-over-for-small-caps-here-s-what-past-data-indicates-118120900518_1.html); [5paisa](https://www.5paisa.com/blog/small-cap-index-30-percent-below-peak-36-percent-trading-days)). Before the outcome, a concentrated founder and a concentrated speculator look the same. Only the survivors get profiled.

### Short records cannot separate luck from skill

Mauboussin's "paradox of skill" is the direct reply to "everyone has read the same books." As more competitors become skilled and similar, the spread of skill narrows and luck decides more of the difference. Ted Williams' .406 season was about four standard deviations above average, and a hitter that far above average today would bat about .380 ([Advisor Perspectives](https://www.advisorperspectives.com/articles/2012/12/11/luck-versus-skill-in-investing-new-insights-from-the-world-of-sports-and-beyond)). Mauboussin and Arbesman studied 5,593 large-cap US funds from 1962 to 2008. They found more long winning streaks than chance predicts, so skill exists, but only 3 streaks of 10+ years ([CXO Advisory](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/investing-expertise/outperformance-streaks-and-mutual-fund-manager-skill)). Damodaran's rule of thumb is to look for 2–3% a year of outperformance for 15 years with low variability before calling it skill ([Damodaran](https://aswathdamodaran.substack.com/p/luck-versus-skill-how-can-you-tell-11-03-12)). A rough calculation: with 2% true alpha and 8% tracking error, it takes about 64 years to be statistically confident, and 16 years even at 4% alpha. A three-year winning streak, yours or an influencer's, proves almost nothing.

### The behavior gap is larger than most stock-picking edges

Investors earn less than the funds they own because they buy after gains and sell after falls. Morningstar measured a gap of about **1.2 points a year** in the US over the ten years to 2025, widening to 2.1 points in the most volatile funds ([Morningstar](https://www.morningstar.com/business/insights/research/mind-the-gap-2025)). In India, Axis MF found equity and hybrid funds returned about **19.1% a year from 2003 to 2022 while the average investor earned about 13.8%** ([Axis MF](https://www.axismf.com/cms/sites/default/files/pdf-factsheets/Thinking%20Aloud%20-%20Using%20your%20Mutual%20Funds%20More%20Effectively.pdf)). Over 30 years, that gap compounds to roughly a 4x difference in ending wealth. Even Lynch's investors lagged him: one estimate puts their dollar-weighted return at 13.4% against the fund's 21.8% over 1981–1990 ([CBS MoneyWatch](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/lessons-from-a-great-fund-managers-record)). Barber and Odean found the most active fifth of traders netted 11.4% a year against 18.5% for the least active, with gross returns similar, so costs ate the difference ([Odean](https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/odean/Papers%20current%20versions/Individual_Investor_Performance_Final.pdf)). Investors also sell winners too early and hold losers too long, and the winners they sell go on to beat the losers they keep ([Odean, 1998](https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/odean/papers/disposition/disposition.html)).

### Great stocks demand surviving drawdowns that leverage makes fatal

Amazon fell about **94% from 1999 to 2001** and took about a decade to regain its peak ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2018/12/18/dotcom-bubble-amazon-stock-lost-more-than-90percent-long-term-investors-still-got-rich.html)). Apple, Microsoft and Nvidia each fell more than 80% at some point ([Irish Times](https://www.irishtimes.com/your-money/2025/06/17/the-agony-and-the-equity-most-stocks-fall-more-than-50-at-some-point)). The median dot-com tech IPO, however, ended down 85% and never recovered ([Lambda Finance](https://www.lambdafin.com/articles/tech-ipo-drawdown-history)). "Hold through the drawdown" is good advice only when you understand the business well enough to tell an Amazon from the median IPO. Recovery arithmetic is unforgiving: a 50% loss needs +100% to break even, and a 90% loss needs +900%. With leverage, a drawdown you could have waited out ends with your position sold for you. That is the thread running from LTCM and Archegos to Buffett's remark that LTCM "risked what they did have and did need" for money they didn't need ([Novel Investor](https://novelinvestor.com/buffetts-lessons-long-term-capital-management/)).

## What this means for you

Start with the uncomfortable conclusion. You are not going to become Jhunjhunwala by reading better, and nobody gets onto a rich list by stock-picking a salary's savings in their twenties. Being realistic about that is not defeatism. It frees you to build the edges that are actually available to you.

**Domain expertise and scuttlebutt.** This is the one information edge an individual still has legally. Lynch's own claim was modest: "the average person could know three or four or five companies very well" ([PBS Frontline](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/pages/frontline/shows/betting/pros/lynch.html)). Buffett's Amex check was literally asking restaurants whether customers still used the card. If you work in pharma distribution, logistics, chemicals, a hospital, a factory or software services, you see order books, pricing, customer behavior and promoter reputations before analysts do. Concentrate your stock-picking there and treat everything outside it as index territory. Domain knowledge is also what makes holding through a 50% fall rational rather than stubborn. Stay on the right side of the law: Jhunjhunwala's Aptech settlement shows where "access" turns into a ₹18.5 crore problem.

**Small and neglected companies.** This is the space institutions cannot use. Buffett's "huge structural advantage not to have a lot of money" applies to you. A fund running thousands of crores cannot build a meaningful position in a ₹500 crore company, and you can. The price of admission is volatility. Porinju's −67% and the 2008 small-cap fall of 68% are what this area does periodically. Size positions so a governance blow-up like LEEL or A2Z costs you a few percent of net worth, not half. And do not buy after a famous investor's bulk deal shows up in the news, because by then you are the exit liquidity.

**A long horizon and capital nobody can redeem.** This is Buffett's real secret in a form you can use. Institutions face quarterly reviews, redemptions and benchmark risk. You face none of those unless you borrow. "Never a fire sale" requires no margin, no loan against shares and an emergency fund, so that a job loss in a bear market does not force you to sell at the bottom.

**Staying out of F&O.** Avoiding a losing game is the easiest edge you will ever get. Close to nine in ten individuals lost money in every year SEBI studied. The profits went to about 700 algorithmic institutions with microsecond execution. Costs alone took ₹25,000 crore a year from individuals. Copying a famous option seller does not change which side of the structure you are on.

**Owning a business.** Damani is the template. The Forbes data says the same thing: most large fortunes are founder equity, a stake worth far more than anyone could have saved. If you want a shot at outsized wealth, a business you run (even a small one) gives you control, information and leverage on your own effort that no listed stock can.

**Savings rate and time.** These matter more than return. The table below is my own illustration. It assumes a monthly SIP with returns compounded monthly. The 12% row is a plausible long-run diversified-equity assumption, not a promise. 15% is what a genuinely skilled investor might sustain. 18% for 30 years is a record almost nobody achieves: Lynch did 29% for only 13 years, and Buffett's partnership 23.8% for 13.

| Monthly SIP | Years | Invested | At 8% | At 12% | At 15% | At 18% |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| ₹10,000 | 10 | ₹12 lakh | ₹18 lakh | ₹22 lakh | ₹26 lakh | ₹31 lakh |
| ₹10,000 | 20 | ₹24 lakh | ₹57 lakh | ₹92 lakh | ₹1.33 cr | ₹1.93 cr |
| ₹10,000 | 30 | ₹36 lakh | ₹1.42 cr | ₹3.08 cr | ₹5.63 cr | ₹10.4 cr |
| ₹25,000 | 20 | ₹60 lakh | ₹1.43 cr | ₹2.30 cr | ₹3.32 cr | ₹4.82 cr |
| ₹25,000 | 30 | ₹90 lakh | ₹3.54 cr | ₹7.70 cr | ₹14.1 cr | ₹26.0 cr |
| ₹50,000 | 30 | ₹1.8 cr | ₹7.09 cr | ₹15.4 cr | ₹28.2 cr | ₹52.0 cr |
| ₹25,000, raised 10% a year | 30 | — | ₹11.6 cr | ₹20.0 cr | ₹31.6 cr | — |

Four lessons follow from the table. First, saving ₹25,000 a month at an ordinary 12% (₹7.7 crore) beats saving ₹10,000 a month at an elite 15% (₹5.6 crore). Your savings rate is a lever you control completely, and alpha is not. Second, raising your SIP 10% a year with your salary nearly triples the outcome, which is worth more than any stock tip. Third, the years from 20 to 30 do most of the work, so starting a decade late costs more than picking badly. Fourth, inflation is real. At 6% a year, ₹1 crore thirty years from now buys roughly what ₹17 lakh buys today, so ₹7.7 crore nominal is a comfortable retirement, not a Forbes listing. A one-time ₹10 lakh at 30% a year for ten years becomes about ₹1.4 crore. That is roughly what an outlier like Porinju's best stretch could deliver, and it is still not generational wealth.

**What to stop doing.** Stop trading weekly and expiry-day options, buying or selling. Stop paying for Telegram, WhatsApp or "course" calls from unregistered advisers: SEBI's orders against Sundar, "Baap of Chart" and Avadhut Sathe show the reliable money flows from followers to the influencer. Stop copying bulk deals and "superstar portfolio" trackers after the price has already moved. Stop using margin or loans against shares, which is how drawdowns become ruin. Stop treating FII selling as stock-specific news. Stop judging anyone, yourself included, on a one-to-three-year record. Stop buying fading businesses because they look cheap: that was Buffett's own repeated mistake. Stop trading in and out of good funds after bad quarters, since the behavior gap of 1–5 points a year is larger than most picking edges. And stop holding a collapsing stock out of hope when you cannot explain why the business is unharmed. Jhunjhunwala's test of "no permanent impairment of value" is the question to ask, and if the honest answer is "I don't know," the position is too big.

## Conclusion

The playbook you read is not wrong. It covers only the visible part of each fortune. Strip each legend down and you find an asset the books cannot hand you. For Buffett it was cheap, uncallable capital and seventy years of time. For Lynch it was access and a workload that burned him out at 46. For Damani it was a company he built. For Jhunjhunwala it was an era, borrowed money, a bear market on the right side and one stock that went up a thousandfold. For Porinju it was a tolerance for losing two-thirds. For Sundar it was an audience. For Jane Street it was speed and balance sheet. Knowledge was the entry ticket, and it has been widely shared for decades. Because everyone now has it, it decides less than ever, which is exactly what the paradox of skill predicts.

The useful reframing is to stop asking "how do I find the next Titan" and start asking which structural advantage you have that a fund or an algorithm does not. For most Indian professionals the honest answer is three things: specialised knowledge of one industry, the freedom to own small companies and hold them for a decade without anyone redeeming you, and the ability to save hard and stay out of the games built to transfer money from individuals to institutions. Built over 25–30 years, those produce real wealth. Extreme wealth still comes mostly from founding something, and from the luck that no amount of reading can manufacture.
